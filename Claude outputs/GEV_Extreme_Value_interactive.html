MLE, block maxima and the GEV shape parameter

Five things to play with, in the order Day 3 was actually taught: how maximum likelihood picks a number, why it needs its own theorem for maxima rather than averages, what the shape parameter ξ physically means, and why its estimate is so much less stable at Surkhet than at Kathmandu. Every curve and number below is computed live from the same fits behind the Day 3 notes.

1MLE, the simplest possible case

Ten coin flips. Drag the slider to change how many came up heads, and watch the likelihood curve — the probability of seeing exactly this data — as a function of the candidate probability p. MLE just picks the p where that curve peaks.

In words: MLE is not a formula you memorise, it is this picture — try every candidate probability, compute how likely your actual, exact data would be under each one, and keep the winner. For 8 heads out of 10, no candidate beats p = 0.8, because 0.8 is the value under which that specific outcome is least surprising. Drag to 4/10: the peak moves to 0.4 with no extra reasoning required. The generalisation used later in this page — 40 heads out of 100 flips gives p̂ = 0.40 — is exactly the same rule at a different sample size, not a new one.

2MLE for continuous data — no single value has a probability

Rainfall depths are not coin flips. A continuous variable's density is tall where data is plausible, low where it isn't — but no exact value ever has a positive probability, only a density. Fit a Normal(μ, σ=1) to three points, {2, 4, 6}. Drag μ and watch the three heights, and their product — the likelihood — change together.

f(2;μ)
f(4;μ)
f(6;μ)
Likelihood L(μ)
In words: at μ = 3, the heights are 0.242, 0.242, 0.0044 — that last one is tiny, because x = 6 sits far out in the tail of a curve centred at 3. At μ = 4 the heights are 0.054, 0.399, 0.054 — no single height is as large as 0.399, but the product wins, about 4.5× higher, because nothing is being badly mispredicted. Calculus (set the derivative of the log-likelihood to zero) proves μ = 4, the sample mean, is the exact maximiser over every candidate — not just a better guess than 3. This is what scipy.stats.<dist>.fit() does numerically instead of by hand, and it is the same search used to produce every μ, σ, ξ in this page.

3Two different theorems for two different summaries

Draw n values from the same skewed distribution (Exponential(1), a stand-in for "some messy real quantity") and compute two different summaries of the block: its average and its maximum. Slide the block size and watch what each summary's own distribution does as n grows — 400 blocks redrawn fresh each time you move the slider.

In words: push n up and the average's histogram does the familiar thing — it tightens around a fixed centre and turns into a symmetric bell (the Central Limit Theorem, CLT). The maximum's histogram does not do that: its centre keeps sliding right forever and it stays right-skewed at every block size. It has its own limit theorem (Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko, FTG) with its own family — the GEV — and its own centring/scaling rule, not the Normal's. Set n = 1 and look at the maximum panel: with a block of size one, the "maximum" is just the raw data point, which is exactly why fitting GEV to raw daily rainfall is a category error — block size 1 is not a maximum of anything, so FTG makes no promise about it at all.

4What ξ actually controls — the three tail regimes

Same GEV shape, standardised to μ = 0, σ = 1, one slider: ξ (xi). This is the 🔭 figure flagged as an open thread in the Day 3 notes — Kathmandu and Surkhet marked as real examples on opposite sides of ξ = 0.

Regime
Upper endpoint
the largest value physically possible
P(X > 6)
how much mass survives into the far tail
In words: ξ = 0 is Gumbel — the tail decays exponentially fast; no hard ceiling, but very large values become rare very quickly. ξ > 0 is Fréchet — the tail decays like a power law, i.e. slowly; no ceiling at all, and large outliers stay far more plausible than Gumbel suggests. ξ < 0 is Weibull (a different Weibull than the reliability-engineering one) — a genuine finite upper bound the variable can mathematically never cross. Kathmandu's ξ ≈ −0.003 sits essentially on the Gumbel line — for practical purposes, no detectable ceiling. Surkhet's ξ ≈ +0.398 is well into Fréchet — a real, heavy tail, and the reason its 1000-year return level (≈1028 mm, computed in Set D below) is more than double its 10-year level, where Kathmandu's is not.

In words: ξ is estimated almost entirely from the top few order statistics, not all 41 points equally. Surkhet has one single year sitting far above every other year, with no competing evidence from other years to outvote it; Kathmandu's top values decrease smoothly, giving the bootstrap resamples nothing comparable to swing on. That gap-to-next-highest is the mechanism — the wide CI is the consequence.

Deleting one point is a different, cruder test than resampling — and the honest result does not hand you the clean story. Kathmandu's own ξ̂ also swings a lot when its single largest year is deleted (−0.003 → −0.219), and here L-moments swing by more than MLE in both deletions, not less. Removing one order statistic from 41 tests something different from what the bootstrap tests: the bootstrap resamples the whole record with replacement thousands of times and asks how much the estimate typically varies; a single deletion just asks what one specific point is worth. L-moments' claimed advantage — a bounded influence function, so no single value can dominate through amplification — is a statement about that resampling behaviour, which is exactly the CI-width comparison above, not about surviving the removal of one point. Running the L-moments estimator through the same bootstrap machinery used for MLE is flagged in the notes' open thread as the next real check, not yet done here.
