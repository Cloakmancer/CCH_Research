## Section 0 — Pre-questions (answer from memory, before anything else)

Write answers to all six now. No notes, no calculator, no scrolling. Ten minutes maximum. You are
*expected* to get some wrong — that is what makes the rest of the set work.

1. Define marginal, joint and conditional probability in one sentence each, using the rainfall
   table above.
2. Which is the law and which is the assumption: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B|A), or P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)?
3. What does U = F(X) do, and for which X does it work?
4. State Sklar's theorem in words. What does it let you do that you could not do otherwise?
5. Two hazards have Kendall's τ = 0.5. Does that tell you how likely they are to be extreme
   *together*? Why or why not?
6. A "100-year joint event" — name the two different things that phrase could mean.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Model answers — open only after writing all six</b></summary>

1. **Marginal**: the probability of one variable ignoring the other — P(R) = 50/500 = 0.10, read
   off a row or column total. **Joint**: both at once — P(R ∩ S) = 32/500 = 0.064, one cell.
   **Conditional**: one *given* the other — P(S|R) = 32/50 = 0.64, one cell divided by its own
   row total rather than by 500.
2. The **first is the law** — it is the definition of P(B|A) rearranged, and always true. The
   **second is the assumption**, true only in the special case P(B|A) = P(B).
3. It maps X onto its own percentile, and the result is **Uniform(0,1)** for *any* continuous X.
   Continuity is the condition: with ties or atoms the transform is no longer exactly uniform.
4. Any joint distribution can be written as **margins plus a copula**, and the two can be chosen
   independently: F(x₁,x₂) = C(F₁(x₁), F₂(x₂)). It lets you argue about marginal shape and about
   dependence structure as **two separate problems**.
5. **No.** τ is a single scalar summarising association over the *whole* distribution. Families
   matched on τ disagree by a factor of two or more in the corner — that is Panel 5's entire
   content.
6. **AND** (both variables exceed their thresholds) or **OR** (either does). At u = 0.99 with a
   Gumbel copula those are 170 years and 71 years — not two estimates of one number, but answers
   to two different engineering questions.

Score yourself out of 6. Whatever you missed, that section of the set is the one to slow down on.
</details>

## Set A — Foundations (recognition and reasoning, no calculator)

### A1 — worked example (read this one; do not skip it)

*From the standing table, compute the two marginals, the joint, and both conditionals. Then say
which of those five numbers a copula is about.*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Worked solution</b></summary>

![Two mosaic plots of the 500 monsoon days. The observed panel puts 32 days in the heavy-rain-and-saturated cell; the independence panel requires only 15. Column widths, which are the margins, are identical in both panels](images/day2-contingency.png)

Work through it in order rather than pattern-matching. Every one of these is a count divided by a
count; the only question each time is **which denominator**.

- **Marginal P(R)** = 50/500 = **0.10.** Denominator: every day. The column total, ignoring
  saturation entirely.
- **Marginal P(S)** = 150/500 = **0.30.** Denominator: every day. The row total.
- **Joint P(R ∩ S)** = 32/500 = **0.064.** Denominator: every day. One cell.
- **Conditional P(S | R)** = 32/50 = **0.64.** Denominator: **the rainy days only.** Same 32 days
  in the numerator as the joint — what changed is the world they are measured against.
- **Conditional P(R | S)** = 32/150 = **0.213.** Same numerator again, different denominator.

**Which one is the copula about?** The **joint**, and only the joint — but stated in a way that
has the margins divided out. Notice that the two marginals are fixed by the row and column totals
and carry no information about the relationship at all: you could have P(R) = 0.10 and P(S) = 0.30
with the joint anywhere from 0 to 0.10. A copula is precisely the machinery for saying *where in
that range the joint sits*, in a form that does not depend on what the margins happened to be.

The transferable move: whenever you meet a probability, **ask what its denominator is**. Marginal
and joint both divide by the whole sample; conditional divides by a subset. Nearly every
probability error in an applied paper is a denominator that quietly changed.
</details>

### A2
Using the standing table, is P(S | R) bigger or smaller than P(S)? By how much, and what does the
comparison mean physically?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Bigger: 0.640 against 0.300**, a risk ratio of 0.640/0.262 = **2.44** against the non-rainy days,
or a lift of 0.064/(0.10 × 0.30) = **2.13** against independence.

![Three stacked bars: all 500 days give P(S) = 0.300; the 50 rainy days give P(S | R) = 0.640; the 450 dry days give P(S | not R) = 0.262](images/day2-conditional-slice.png)

Physically: **heavy rain and saturated soil arrive together more often than chance**, which is
exactly what you would expect from a catchment where a wet spell both saturates the ground and
delivers the storm. The number matters because a landslide-triggering model that assumes rainfall
and antecedent saturation are independent will underestimate how often the dangerous combination
occurs — here by a factor of about 2.1, before any cascade multiplies it.
</details>

### A3
Explain why P(S | R) = 0.64 and P(R | S) = 0.213 are both correct, and why confusing them is the
single most common probability error in applied work.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

They answer **different questions with the same 32 days**, differing only in the denominator:
32/50 versus 32/150. Both are correct; neither is "the" probability that rain and saturation go
together.

The confusion is common because natural language does not distinguish them. *"Rain and saturation
are linked"* is compatible with either. The costly version in hazard work: **P(landslide | extreme
rain) is not P(extreme rain | landslide)**. The first is what a warning system needs — it is small,
because most heavy rain does not produce a landslide. The second is near 1, because most landslides
do follow heavy rain, and quoting it as though it were the first makes a useless alarm look like a
good one.

🔭 **[FUTURE]** This is the base-rate fallacy, and its formal cure is Bayes' theorem (A5). It
returns as a real problem when the project starts evaluating trigger thresholds against observed
events.
</details>

### A4
True or false, with a reason for each:
(a) If P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B) then A and B are independent.
(b) If two variables are independent, their conditional probabilities equal their marginals.
(c) Independence and "uncorrelated" are the same thing.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

(a) **True — this is the definition**, not a consequence of one. Independence is *defined* as the
joint factorising into the marginals; everything else about independence is derived from it.

(b) **True, and it is the more useful form.** P(B|A) = P(A∩B)/P(A) = P(A)P(B)/P(A) = P(B).
Independence means **knowing A tells you nothing about B** — the conditional collapses onto the
marginal. If you can only remember one statement of independence, remember this one; it is the one
you can check against intuition.

(c) **False, and the distinction is load-bearing here.** Independence is a statement about the
entire joint distribution; zero correlation is a statement about one number. Independence implies
zero correlation, never the reverse. Two variables can have correlation 0 and be strongly
dependent — and, more relevantly for this project, two variables can have *identical* correlation
and completely different tail behaviour, which is Set C.
</details>

### A5
Derive Bayes' theorem from the multiplication rule in two lines, then verify it on the standing
table.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Two routes to the same joint probability: P(R) times P(S given R), and P(S) times P(R given S), both arriving at 0.064](images/day2-bayes.png)

The joint factors **two ways**, because "A then B" and "B then A" describe the same pair of days:

$$P(R)\,P(S|R) \;=\; P(R \cap S) \;=\; P(S)\,P(R|S)$$

Divide the outer equality by P(S):

$$P(R|S) \;=\; \frac{P(S|R)\,P(R)}{P(S)}$$

**Verify:** 0.640 × 0.10 / 0.30 = **0.2133** = 32/150 ✓. And the joint from each side:
0.10 × 0.640 = 0.064, and 0.30 × 0.213 = 0.064 ✓.

The point worth carrying: **Bayes is an algebraic rearrangement, not a separate law.** There is
nothing about belief in the derivation. It is the observation that a joint probability does not
care which variable you condition on first.
</details>

### A6
With P(R) = 0.10 and P(S) = 0.30 held fixed, what is the full range the joint P(R ∩ S) could take?
What does the answer tell you about how much information the margins carry?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![A line showing the admissible range of P(R and S) from 0.000 to 0.100, with independence marked at 0.030 and the observed value at 0.064](images/day2-frechet.png)

**From max(0, 0.10 + 0.30 − 1) = 0 to min(0.10, 0.30) = 0.10.** The lower bound is forced by the
cells having to stay non-negative; the upper bound by the joint never exceeding either marginal.
The observed 0.064 sits **64% of the way up** that range; independence sits at 0.030, i.e. 30% up.

**What it tells you:** a 2×2 table has four cells, but two margin constraints plus the total leave
exactly **one free number**. The margins carry *none* of the information about association — they
constrain it to an interval and say nothing about where inside it the truth lies.

🔭 **[FUTURE]** Those two bounds are the **Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds**, W and M, and they hold in
exactly this form for continuous copulas: W(u,v) = max(0, u+v−1) ≤ C(u,v) ≤ min(u,v) = M(u,v). The
2×2 table is the whole of copula theory with the continuum removed.
</details>

## Set B — Mechanics (get the arithmetic reliable)

*Scaffolded: B1 shows the steps, later ones do not.*

### B1 — partly worked
From the standing table, compute P(R ∩ S), P(S | R), P(R | S), and the lift over independence.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Step 1 — the joint.** 32/500 = **0.064**.
**Step 2 — the conditionals.** Divide the same cell by each margin's count:
32/50 = **0.640** and 32/150 = **0.2133**.
**Step 3 — what independence would give.** P(R)·P(S) = 0.10 × 0.30 = **0.030**, i.e. 15 days.
**Step 4 — the lift.** 0.064/0.030 = **2.133×**.

So the dangerous combination happens **a little over twice as often as chance**, and the expected
count under independence (15) is less than half what was observed (32).
</details>

### B2
Compute the odds ratio for the standing table and say what it adds that the lift does not.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Odds ratio = (32 × 332)/(18 × 118) = 10,624/2,124 = **5.00**.

What it adds: the odds ratio is **invariant to the marginals**. Rescale either margin — study a
wetter year with more rainy days — and the lift changes while the odds ratio does not. That is
precisely the property a measure of association needs, and precisely the property a copula is
built to have in the continuous case. The lift is easier to explain; the odds ratio is the one
that compares across catchments.
</details>

### B3
The expected counts under independence are 15, 35, 135, 315. Compute the χ² statistic and comment
on whether the association could plausibly be chance.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(32-15)^2}{15}+\frac{(18-35)^2}{35}+\frac{(118-135)^2}{135}+\frac{(332-315)^2}{315}$$

= 19.267 + 8.257 + 2.141 + 0.917 = **30.58** on 1 degree of freedom, p ≈ **3.2 × 10⁻⁸**.

No, it could not plausibly be chance. Note where the evidence comes from: the **smallest** cell
contributes 19.3 of the 30.6, because χ² weights by 1/expected. The rare corner is doing the work
— a foreshadowing of every tail-dependence argument in this set.

**But note what the test does not say.** It rejects independence. It says nothing about *where* in
the admissible range the joint sits, nothing about which dependence structure generated it, and —
if these were continuous variables — nothing whatever about the tail. 🔭 **[FUTURE]** That gap
between "not independent" and "this particular dependence structure" is what copula family
selection exists to close.
</details>

### B6
Convert between τ and the family parameters at τ = 0.5, for all three families. Then do τ = 0.3.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

The three conversions worth having automatic:

$$\theta_{\text{Gumbel}} = \frac{1}{1-\tau}, \qquad
\theta_{\text{Clayton}} = \frac{2\tau}{1-\tau}, \qquad
\rho_{\text{Gauss}} = \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{2}\right)$$

| τ | Gumbel θ | λ_U | Clayton θ | λ_L | Gaussian ρ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 1.429 | 0.3755 | 0.857 | 0.4454 | 0.4540 |
| 0.5 | 2.000 | **0.5858** | 2.000 | **0.7071** | 0.7071 |

At τ = 0.5 the Gumbel and Clayton parameters coincide at 2 — a numerical accident of these two
formulae, not a statement that the copulas resemble each other. Their tail behaviour is opposite.
</details>

### B7
Compute λ_U for Gumbel and λ_L for Clayton at τ = 0.5, from the formulas
λ_U = 2 − 2^(1/θ) and λ_L = 2^(−1/θ).

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

- Gumbel, θ = 2: λ_U = 2 − 2^0.5 = 2 − 1.4142 = **0.5858**. λ_L = 0.
- Clayton, θ = 2: λ_L = 2^(−0.5) = **0.7071**. λ_U = 0.
- Gaussian, ρ = 0.7071: λ_U = λ_L = **0**.

Clayton's lower-tail dependence is *stronger* than Gumbel's upper — 0.707 against 0.586 — and
entirely useless for a flood cascade, because it binds the variables when both are **small**. A
family can have plenty of tail dependence and still be the wrong family.
</details>

### B8
At u = 0.99 both, compute P(both exceed) and hence the AND return period under (a) independence
and (b) a Gumbel copula with τ = 0.5. Predict the ratio before you compute it.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

The identity to know:

$$P(U_1>u,\,U_2>u) \;=\; 1 - 2u + C(u,u)$$

(a) **Independence.** C(u,u) = u² = 0.9801. P = 1 − 1.98 + 0.9801 = 0.0001, so
T_AND = **10,000 years**. (Which you could have written down: 0.01 × 0.01.)

(b) **Gumbel.** C(u,u) = u^(2^(1/θ)) = 0.99^1.4142 = 0.985885. P = 1 − 1.98 + 0.985885 =
0.005885, so T_AND = **170 years**.

**Ratio: 58.9×.** Independence calls a 170-year event a 10,000-year event. If your prediction was
"a factor of two or three", that gap is the lesson of the set.

![Joint AND return period on a log scale against the univariate quantile u, for Gumbel, Gaussian, Clayton and independence; at u = 0.99 the independence curve sits 59 times above the Gumbel one](images/day2-joint-return.png)

*Note the shape, not just the endpoint: the four curves are close at u = 0.80 and fan apart without
bound as u → 1. The error is small where you have data and largest exactly where the design value
is read — Day 1's argument, now in two dimensions.*
</details>

## Set C — The core argument (comparison and inference)

### C1
Explain, using the Sklar figure, what changes and what does not when you swap the margins.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Three scatter plots: the same 1,200 copula points shown on the unit square in the middle, and mapped through two different pairs of margins on either side](images/day2-sklar.png)

**What changes:** the physical scatter — its units, its axis ranges, the visual shape of the cloud,
and every quantity computed from the raw numbers (means, correlations of the raw values, design
values in m³/s).

**What does not change:** the middle panel. Not approximately — *exactly*, point for point. Because
F is monotone, applying it cannot reorder anything; each observation keeps its rank in both
variables, and the unit-square scatter is nothing but those ranks.

**Why this is the whole game:** it means the question "what are the margins?" and the question
"how do the variables move together?" can be answered **separately, by different people, with
different data, using different methods**, and neither answer contaminates the other. Sklar's
theorem is the licence for that separation.
</details>

### C2
The margins are right-skewed and the raw scatter is a fan. After the transform the scatter fills
the unit square evenly. Has information been lost? Say exactly what.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Yes, and precisely one thing: all magnitude information within each variable.** After the
transform, the largest peak on record is merely "u = 0.997" — one notch above the second largest,
indistinguishable in kind. The data no longer knows whether the biggest flood was twice the median
or twenty times it.

**What survives is the joint ordering**, which is all the copula needs: whether the high-peak years
are the *same years* as the high-volume years is a statement about ranks alone.

**The consequence, stated exactly:** a copula can tell you how *often* peak and volume are jointly
extreme, and never how *big* the resulting event is in m³/s. Recovering that requires putting the
margins back — which is why margin choice stays a live problem no matter how good the copula is
(Day 1, C3; and D4 below).
</details>

### C3
Four families are fitted to the same data, all with τ = 0.5. Compute P(U₂ > u | U₁ > u) at
u = 0.90, 0.95, 0.99 for each, and describe the pattern.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Four curves of the conditional probability against the quantile cutoff: Gumbel flattening onto 0.586, Gaussian bending toward zero, Clayton and Independence both collapsing](images/day2-tail-dependence-families.png)

| u | Gumbel | Gaussian | Clayton | Independence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.90 | 0.6157 | 0.4739 | 0.2503 | 0.1000 |
| 0.95 | 0.6006 | 0.3985 | 0.1364 | 0.0500 |
| 0.99 | 0.5887 | 0.2735 | 0.0294 | 0.0100 |

**The pattern:** Gumbel is essentially **flat** — it converges to λ_U = 0.5858 and stays there.
Every other family **declines toward zero**, at different speeds. Independence declines linearly
(it is just 1 − u). Clayton declines fastest of the dependent families, because all of its
dependence is in the opposite corner.

The substantive point is not that the numbers differ. It is that they differ **more the further out
you look** — 1.30× between Gumbel and Gaussian at u = 0.90, 2.15× at u = 0.99 — while the single
summary statistic everyone reports, τ, is identical for all four.
</details>

### C4
"The Gaussian copula misses 54% of joint extremes at u = 0.99." Reconstruct that number and state
carefully what it is a percentage *of*.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

From C3 at u = 0.99: Gumbel 0.5887, Gaussian 0.2735. Then 1 − 0.2735/0.5887 = **0.536 ≈ 54%**.

**What it is a percentage of:** the joint extremes that the **Gumbel model** predicts — not of
some observed truth. The honest statement is *"if the Gumbel model is right, a Gaussian copula
fitted to the same τ will report only 46% of the joint exceedances at this level."* It is a
comparison between two models, and it becomes a claim about reality only once you have an argument
for Gumbel — which for peak and volume is physical (they come out of the same storm), not
statistical.

Getting this qualifier right matters more than the number. A sentence that says "the Gaussian
copula underestimates joint extremes by 54%" full stop is overclaiming, and it is the kind of
overclaim a reviewer will find.
</details>

### C5
Independence is a copula too — the product copula Π(u,v) = uv. Where does it sit relative to the
other three, and what does that make it useful for?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

It sits **at the bottom of C3's table at every cutoff**, below all three dependent families. It is
also, from A6, one specific point inside the Fréchet–Hoeffding interval — not the middle, and not
a boundary.

**What it is useful for: it is the null model.** "These hazards compound" is a claim, and the thing
it is a claim *against* is Π. Quoting a joint return period without also quoting the independence
value hides the entire size of the effect — which is why the ratio in B8 (58.9×), not the 170
years alone, is the number worth reporting.

There is a second, less obvious use: Π is the copula you get when the rank transform is applied to
two genuinely unrelated variables, so it is also the reference distribution for testing whether an
apparent association could have arisen by chance in a short record.
</details>

### C6
Why is Kendall's τ a poor basis for choosing a copula family, when it is a perfectly good measure
of association?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Because it is **one number summarising a whole surface**, and it is computed over all the data,
which means it is dominated by the body. C3's four families are *by construction* identical in τ
and differ by a factor of 2.15 in the corner. Matching τ pins the average level of association and
says nothing about where that association is concentrated.

The generalisation is worth stating in full, because it recurs: **any global summary statistic —
τ, Spearman's ρ_S, a Pearson correlation, an AIC computed over the full sample — is blind to the
tail by construction**, since the tail contains almost none of the data. For a Gaussian copula at
ρ = 0.7071, τ = 0.500 and ρ_S = 0.690; both are stable, well-estimated, and completely silent about
the corner.

🔭 **[FUTURE]** The alternatives are tail-focused: empirical λ̂_U(u) plotted over u ∈ [0.9, 0.99]
against candidate families, and Cramér–von Mises with a parametric bootstrap. Both are already in
the project plan; E5 is about why even they are hard on 40 years of data.
</details>

## Set D — The CCH bridge (why this matters for *this* project)

### D1
Write out, in five steps, the path from "n hazard variables with different distributions" to "a
fitted copula". Name what could go wrong at each step.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

1. **Fit a margin to each variable separately.** Rainfall might be Gamma, discharge GEV, soil
   saturation Beta. *What goes wrong:* margin misspecification — a contested choice (GEV vs LP3 vs
   log-normal) that changes design values by a factor of 1.58 at u = 0.99 (D4).
2. **Transform each: u_i = F_i(x_i).** *What goes wrong:* ties and atoms break the exact
   uniformity — dry days at zero rainfall are a real instance of this, not a theoretical worry.
3. **Assemble the pseudo-observations (u₁,…,u_n).** *What goes wrong:* using n rather than n+1 as
   the divisor puts the largest observation at exactly 1.0, asserting you have seen the physical
   maximum.
4. **Fit a copula to those, choosing a family.** *What goes wrong:* family selection on a global
   criterion, which C6 shows is blind to the region that matters.
5. **Read the answer back out** — λ_U, a joint return period, a design pair in physical units.
   *What goes wrong:* step 5 re-invokes the margins from step 1, so the error you isolated in the
   dependence estimate comes back in the design value.

Steps 1–2 are Panel 3 of the interactive page. Step 3–4 are Panel 4. Step 5 is Panel 6.
</details>

### D2
A reservoir fails only if a high peak arrives **and** the volume is large enough to sustain it. A
levee fails if the peak alone overtops it. At u = 0.90 under a Gumbel copula, give the return
period each engineer should quote.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Two unit squares showing the same 3,000 events: the AND region is the top-right corner box, the OR region is the union of two bands](images/day2-and-or.png)

- **Reservoir — AND.** P = 1 − 2(0.90) + C(0.90,0.90) = 1 − 1.8 + 0.86156 = 0.0616 →
  **T = 16.2 years**.
- **Levee — OR.** P = 1 − C(0.90,0.90) = 1 − 0.86156 = 0.1384 → **T = 7.2 years**.

Same data, same copula, same u, and **a factor of 2.2 between them**. These are not two estimates
of one quantity to be reconciled or averaged; they are answers to two different questions, and the
right one is determined by the **failure mechanism**, not by the statistics. Quoting the AND period
for a levee is not conservative — it is wrong, and wrong in the unsafe direction.

🔭 **[FUTURE]** There is a third definition, the **Kendall return period**, for structures whose
response integrates both drivers without a discrete threshold. The project's own
[AND/OR decision tool](https://cloakmancer.github.io/CCH_Research/interactive/and-or-decision.html)
walks the choice.
</details>

### D3
Three cascade links, each requiring its variable to exceed u = 0.99. Compute the chain probability
under independence and under λ_U-chaining, and comment on how the error scales with chain length.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Three chained boxes — rainfall, discharge, slope failure — each above u = 0.99, showing one in 291 years when chained by tail dependence against one in a million under independence](images/day2-cascade-chain.png)

- **Independence:** 0.01 × 0.01 × 0.01 = 10⁻⁶ → **1 in 1,000,000 years**.
- **Chained by λ_U:** 0.01 × 0.5858 × 0.5858 = 3.431 × 10⁻³ → **1 in 291 years**.
- **Ratio: 3,431×.**

**How it scales:** each additional link multiplies the discrepancy by another λ_U/(1−u) — here
0.5858/0.01 ≈ 58.6. Two links are wrong by ~59×, three by ~3,431×, four by ~200,000×. The error is
not merely large; it is **exponential in the number of links**.

This is the sharpest statement of why the copula machinery is not decoration on this project. A
single-hazard analysis that assumes independence where none exists is wrong by a bounded factor. A
*cascading* analysis is wrong by that factor raised to the length of the chain — and cascades are
the object of study.
</details>

### D4
The bridge note says an incorrectly light-tailed marginal "will understate λ and understate
compounding risk". Sharpen that statement — it is nearly right and the correction is instructive.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Left: the same u = 0.99 read back through four candidate margins gives design values from 1,824 to 2,876 m³/s. Right: lambda_U is 0.586 under all four](images/day2-margin-vs-copula.png)

**The correction:** λ_U is a property of the **copula alone** and is *invariant* to the margins —
that invariance is exactly Sklar's theorem (C1). Changing the marginal family cannot change the
true λ_U. Read the figure: four candidate margins for Q put the u = 0.99 design value anywhere from
1,824 to 2,876 m³/s, a spread of **1.58×**, while λ_U sits at 0.5858 in every one of them.

**What is true, and is what the note is reaching for**, is two separate things:

1. **The λ_U *estimate* is contaminated if the margins are parametric.** Under IFM you fit margins
   first and feed F̂_i(x_i) to the copula; if F̂ is misspecified, the pseudo-observations are
   systematically distorted and λ̂_U absorbs a marginal error as though it were dependence. This is
   precisely the argument for **semiparametric (pseudo-ML) fitting on empirical ranks** — which is
   already this project's adopted default.
2. **The design value in physical units depends on the margin regardless.** Step 5 of D1: getting
   λ_U perfectly right buys you a correct *percentile*, and the percentile still has to be
   converted back through a marginal you may have chosen wrongly.

So the risk is real and the conclusion — margins must be got right separately — is correct. But
the mechanism is *estimation* and *back-transformation*, not λ_U itself moving. Worth fixing in
the write-up, because a reviewer who knows copulas will read "misspecified margins change λ_U" as
a misunderstanding of Sklar.
</details>

### D5
Where in the CCH workflow does *marginal* probability appear, where does *joint*, and where does
*conditional*? One sentence each.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

- **Marginal** — the per-variable distribution fits (GEV/Gamma/log-normal for rainfall, discharge,
  saturation), each estimated on its own data and used to build the transform u_i = F_i(x_i).
- **Joint** — the copula C(u₁,…,u_n) itself, and every joint return period read off it: the AND
  and OR design events, the critical layer.
- **Conditional** — the quantity that actually decides a cascade: λ_U = lim P(U₂ > u | U₁ > u),
  the probability that link *k+1* fires given that link *k* has, which is what turns a set of
  independent hazards into a chain.

The one-line version, worth memorising because it is the argument of the whole day: **margins are
one variable at a time, the copula is all of them at once, and tail dependence is the conditional
that makes a cascade a cascade.**
</details>

## Set E — Hard (synthesis and derivation)

### E1
Prove that U = F(X) is Uniform(0,1) for continuous, strictly increasing F. Then say exactly where
the proof fails for a discrete variable, and why that matters for rainfall.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

For u ∈ (0,1):

$$P(U \le u) = P(F(X) \le u) = P(X \le F^{-1}(u)) = F(F^{-1}(u)) = u$$

The second step needs F strictly increasing, so F⁻¹ exists and applying it preserves the
inequality. A cdf equal to u on the whole of (0,1) *is* the Uniform(0,1) cdf. ∎

**Where it fails for a discrete variable:** F⁻¹ is not well defined — F jumps, so there are values
of u that no x attains, and P(U ≤ u) becomes a step function rather than u. The transformed
variable is not uniform; it is uniform-ish with gaps at the jumps.

**Why this matters for rainfall specifically:** daily rainfall has an **atom at zero** — a
substantial fraction of days record exactly 0 mm. That is a genuine discrete jump in an otherwise
continuous variable, so the transform is *not* exactly uniform on a raw daily rainfall series, and
ties at zero break rank-based estimators too.

🔭 **[FUTURE]** The standard treatments are to model the mixed distribution explicitly (a point
mass at zero plus a continuous part), to work with wet-day amounts conditional on rain, or to
jitter the ties randomly. Which one is chosen is a real decision for the Nepal rainfall series in
`04_Resources/`, not a technicality.
</details>

### E2
Derive P(U₁ > u, U₂ > u) = 1 − 2u + C(u,u) from the inclusion–exclusion principle, and hence the
expression for λ_U.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Both exceeding is the complement of *at least one* failing to exceed:

$$P(U_1>u, U_2>u) = 1 - P(U_1 \le u \cup U_2 \le u)$$

Inclusion–exclusion on the union:

$$P(U_1 \le u \cup U_2 \le u) = u + u - C(u,u)$$

since each marginal event has probability u and their intersection is C(u,u) by definition. Hence
**1 − 2u + C(u,u)**. ∎

For λ_U, divide by P(U₁ > u) = 1 − u to condition, and take the limit:

$$\lambda_U = \lim_{u \to 1^-} \frac{1 - 2u + C(u,u)}{1-u}$$

For Gumbel, C(u,u) = u^(2^(1/θ)); writing a = 2^(1/θ) and applying L'Hôpital,
the limit is (−2 + a·u^(a−1))|_{u=1} / (−1) = **2 − a = 2 − 2^(1/θ)** ✓, which is 0.5858 at θ = 2.
</details>

### E3
Show that for the Gaussian copula λ_U = 0 for every ρ < 1, yet at u = 0.90 it still gives 0.474.
Reconcile these and explain why the zero is treacherous rather than simply wrong.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Why the limit is zero:** conditional on Z₁ = z, Z₂ is Normal with mean ρz and variance 1 − ρ².
As the threshold z → ∞, the conditional mean ρz falls behind the threshold z by (1 − ρ)z → ∞,
while the conditional spread stays fixed at √(1 − ρ²). The second variable is left further and
further behind in units of its own standard deviation, so the conditional exceedance probability
→ 0 for any ρ < 1.

**Reconciliation:** λ_U = 0 is an *asymptotic* statement and says nothing about any finite cutoff.
The approach is very slow: 0.474 at u = 0.90, 0.399 at 0.95, 0.274 at 0.99, 0.165 at 0.999 — still
visibly non-zero, still heading down.

**Why treacherous:** at the cutoffs you can actually check against data — 0.90, perhaps 0.95, since
you have decades not millennia of record — the Gaussian copula looks reasonable and passes
diagnostics. Its error only becomes stark in the region you cannot check empirically, which is
exactly the region a design decision extrapolates into. **The model is defensible everywhere you
can test it and wrong where you must rely on it.** That is the same structural trap as Day 1's
body-versus-tail argument, one dimension up.
</details>

### E4
A 40-year record. Estimate λ_U empirically at u = 0.95. How many data points does the estimator
actually use, what is its standard error, and can it distinguish Gumbel from Gaussian?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Gumbel and Gaussian conditional-probability curves with binomial error bars at u = 0.90 and 0.95 for a 40-year record; the error bars dwarf the gap between the families](images/day2-record-resolution.png)

- **Points used:** only those exceeding u in the conditioning variable — n(1 − u) = 40 × 0.05 =
  **2**.
- **Standard error:** the estimator is a binomial proportion on those 2 points, so
  s.e. = √(p(1−p)/k) = √(0.6006 × 0.3994 / 2) = **±0.346**.
- **The gap it must resolve:** 0.6006 − 0.3985 = **0.202**.

**No — it cannot distinguish them.** The standard error is 1.7 times the gap. At u = 0.99 it is
worse: 0.4 expected points, i.e. most 40-year records contain **none at all**.

The uncomfortable general statement: **λ_U is defined by a limit that no realistic hydrological
record can approach.** It takes about 1,000 years of annual maxima to get the standard error at
u = 0.99 down to ±0.156, still comparable to the 0.315 gap. So family choice cannot be settled
empirically from a single station, and must lean on physical reasoning (peak and volume come from
one storm), on regional pooling, or on carrying several families through and reporting the spread.
That is not a counsel of despair — it is the honest scope of the claim, and stating it is what
separates the analysis from an overconfident one.
</details>

### E5
Two variables have Kendall's τ = 0.5. Give a bound on how much the joint exceedance probability at
u = 0.99 can differ across all copulas consistent with that τ. Comment on what this means for
reporting.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Within the four families here, at u = 0.99: Clayton gives P = 0.000294, Gumbel gives 0.005887 — a
factor of **20** between two families that agree exactly on τ, on both margins, and on every
summary a reader is likely to be shown.

The true bound over *all* copulas with τ = 0.5 is wider still: the Fréchet–Hoeffding upper bound
M(u,u) = u gives P = 1 − 2u + u = 1 − u = 0.01, so λ_U can be as high as 1, while a
countermonotonic-in-the-tail construction can drive it to 0. **τ constrains the joint exceedance
probability at u = 0.99 essentially not at all.**

**For reporting:** a joint return period quoted without naming the copula family is not a result.
It is one number drawn from a range spanning at least an order of magnitude, and the family
assumption — not the data — is doing most of the work. The defensible format is a table of design
values across 2–3 families with the spread shown, which is already the project's plan.
</details>

### E6
Independence is one point in the Fréchet–Hoeffding interval. Is the *midpoint* of that interval
meaningful? Explain what this tells you about "assuming a moderate dependence" as a default.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**No.** For the standing table the interval is [0, 0.10] with a midpoint of 0.05, while
independence sits at 0.030 and the observation at 0.064. The midpoint corresponds to no
distinguished statistical object — it is an artefact of the margins, and it moves if the margins
move even when the association does not.

**What this tells you about "assume moderate dependence":** it is not a conservative default,
because there is no natural scale on which to be moderate. The only two privileged points in the
interval are **Π (independence)**, which is the null hypothesis, and the **bounds W and M**, which
are the extremes of comonotonicity. Everything between them is an empirical question.

The practical corollary: if you cannot estimate the dependence, say so and report the range
Π-to-M, rather than splitting the difference and implying a precision you do not have. A stated
range is a result; an unstated assumption in the middle of one is a guess wearing a number.
</details>

### E7
Explain why the project fits copulas semiparametrically rather than by IFM, in terms of Day 2's
vocabulary rather than Day 1's.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Day 1 argued this from marginal shape. Day 2 lets it be said in terms of the transform.

Every copula method needs pseudo-observations u_i. There are two ways to make them:

- **IFM:** u_i = F̂_i(x_i) using a *fitted parametric* margin. This is the true probability integral
  transform if and only if F̂_i is the correct distribution. If it is not, the u_i are not uniform,
  and the copula is fitted to distorted inputs — so θ̂ absorbs a marginal error and there is no way
  afterwards to say how much of the estimated dependence was really a bad margin.
- **Semiparametric:** u_i = R(x_i)/(n+1) using **empirical ranks**. This assumes nothing about
  marginal shape and is exactly uniform by construction. θ̂ is then immune to margin
  misspecification.

**Why the trade is right here:** margin choice for flood data is genuinely contested — GEV vs LP3
vs GLO, with different national standards mandating different answers. Committing to a contested
choice in a way that silently poisons a second parameter is exactly the mistake to avoid. Better to
isolate the contested decision so its consequences stay visible, which is D4's point restated.

**The honest cost:** empirical margins are non-smooth and, at the very tail, noisier than a
parametric fit. You buy robustness in θ̂ with some efficiency — and you still need a parametric
margin at step 5 to state a design value in m³/s. Semiparametric fitting removes the contamination,
not the problem.
</details>

### E8
Under a Gumbel copula at u = 0.99, P(both extreme) = 0.00589 and P(V extreme | Q extreme) = 0.589.
The two differ by exactly 100×. Explain why, and name the error this pre-empts.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

They differ by the factor P(Q > 0.99) = 0.01, i.e. **100×**, because the conditional is the joint
divided by the conditioning event's probability — the same denominator move as A1, now in the tail:

$$P(V\text{ ext} \mid Q\text{ ext}) = \frac{P(\text{both ext})}{P(Q\text{ ext})}
= \frac{0.00589}{0.01} = 0.589$$

**The error this pre-empts:** reading λ_U as a return period. λ_U = 0.586 does **not** mean "joint
extremes happen 59% of the time" or "once every 1.7 years". It means: *of the years in which the
peak is extreme — which is 1 year in 100 — about 59% also have an extreme volume.* The joint event
is 1 in 170 years; the conditional is 0.589. One is a frequency, the other is a proportion of a
rare subset.

This is the single most likely misreading of every λ_U in the project's write-up, and it is the
Day 1 mean-versus-median confusion in a new costume: two correct numbers describing different
populations, where quoting the wrong one is an order-of-magnitude error rather than a subtle one.
</details>

## Set G — Misconception clinic (find the flaw)

*Each statement is wrong or misleading in a specific, nameable way. Say what, precisely.*

### G1
*"We tested rainfall and discharge and rejected independence at p < 10⁻⁷, so we are justified in
using a Gumbel copula."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Rejecting independence licenses **"not Π"**, and nothing more. The test in B3 has one degree of
freedom and compares against a single alternative — it cannot distinguish Gumbel from Gaussian from
Clayton, all three of which would reject independence just as decisively on the same data.

Worse, the χ² statistic is a global summary, so it is subject to C6: it is dominated by the body
and blind to the corner where the families actually differ. A tiny p-value against independence is
compatible with a copula whose tail behaviour is the exact opposite of Gumbel's — Clayton at
τ = 0.5 would produce an overwhelming rejection of independence while asserting λ_U = 0.

**The honest chain:** the test rules out one model; the *physics* (peak and volume come from one
storm) argues for upper-tail dependence; the tail diagnostics test that argument as far as the
record allows, which E4 shows is not very far.
</details>

### G2
*"Peak and volume have τ = 0.5 in our data and τ = 0.5 in the published study, so the two
catchments have the same dependence structure."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Matching a summary statistic is not matching a model** (C6). τ is one number; the copula is a
surface. Two catchments can agree exactly on τ and disagree by a factor of 20 in the joint
exceedance probability at u = 0.99 (E5) — and it is the corner, not the average, that both
analyses exist to compute.

The second, quieter error: **τ is estimated, not observed.** On a 40-year record τ̂ carries a
standard error of roughly 0.1, so "0.5 and 0.5" is really "0.5 ± 0.1 and 0.5 ± 0.1", which is
consistent with true values from 0.3 to 0.7 — a range over which λ_U for Gumbel runs from 0.376 to
0.769. The agreement is weaker than it looks even on its own terms.
</details>

### G3
*"The joint return period of the design event is 170 years, so the structure has a 1-in-170 chance
of failing each year."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Three problems, in increasing order of seriousness.

1. **Which joint return period?** 170 years is the **AND** value. The OR value for the same u and
   copula is 71 years. Without naming the failure mechanism the number is undefined (D2).
2. **Return period is not a failure probability.** 1/170 is the annual probability that the
   *hazard* occurs. Whether the structure fails also involves its capacity, its condition and the
   consequences — hazard is not risk.
3. **The number is family-conditional.** Under a Gaussian copula the same u gives 366 years, under
   independence 10,000. Quoting 170 without the family assumption presents a modelling choice as a
   measurement (E5).

The version that survives review: *"under a Gumbel copula fitted at τ = 0.5, the AND joint
exceedance of the 100-year peak and the 100-year volume has an estimated return period of 170
years, against 366 under a Gaussian and 10,000 under independence."*
</details>

### G4
*"Clayton has strong tail dependence — λ = 0.707, higher than Gumbel's 0.586 — so it is the more
conservative choice for a flood cascade."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Right corner, wrong end.** Clayton's 0.707 is λ_**L**, its *lower*-tail dependence: it binds the
variables when both are **small**. Its upper-tail dependence is **zero**. Using Clayton for a flood
cascade means asserting that simultaneously extreme peak and volume become asymptotically
independent — the same claim as the Gaussian copula, dressed in a larger number.

Check it against C3: at u = 0.99, Clayton gives 0.029, against the Gaussian's 0.274 and Gumbel's
0.589. It is not merely *not* conservative; it is the **worst** of the four for this application,
including being worse than the model everyone agrees is wrong.

The general lesson: **"has tail dependence" is not a property, it is half a property.** Always ask
which tail. For a drought study — two variables both extremely low — Clayton is the right family
and Gumbel the wrong one, for exactly symmetric reasons.
</details>

### G5
*"We used the empirical CDF for the margins, so our analysis makes no distributional assumptions
and the design value is assumption-free."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

The first clause is roughly true and the second does not follow. The empirical cdf makes no
parametric assumption, which is why it is the right input to the **copula** (E7). But an empirical
cdf **has no data beyond the observed record** — u = R/(n+1) maxes out at n/(n+1), which for 40
years is 0.976.

So a design value at u = 0.99 cannot be read off the empirical margin at all. Getting there
requires extrapolation, extrapolation requires a parametric tail, and D4's figure shows that choice
moving the answer by 1.58×. The assumption was not avoided; it was **postponed to step 5 and then
not stated**.

The accurate claim is narrower and still worth making: *"the dependence parameter is estimated
without a marginal assumption; the conversion of the design percentile back to m³/s uses a fitted
GEV margin, and the sensitivity of the design value to that choice is reported separately."*
</details>

## Set I — Interleaved mixed set (no labels, no order)

*Do these in one sitting, cold, at least a day after the rest. Deliberately unsorted, and
deliberately mixed with Day 1 — part of the task is recognising which idea each one needs.*

**I1.** A station reports P(landslide | extreme rain) = 0.04 and P(extreme rain | landslide) = 0.91.
A colleague says the numbers are inconsistent. Are they?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Perfectly consistent** — they have different denominators (A3). Bayes relates them:
P(rain|slide) = P(slide|rain)·P(rain)/P(slide). With P(extreme rain) = 0.10 the implied landslide
rate is P(slide) = 0.04 × 0.10 / 0.91 = **0.0044**, about 4 landslide days per thousand. Nothing is
inconsistent; the two numbers simply describe different populations.

The habit worth building: **when two conditionals look contradictory, compute the third quantity
that links them** rather than arguing about which is right. If the implied marginal comes out
absurd — negative, or above 1 — *then* something is wrong.
</details>

**I2.** Under the standing margins, a year records Q = 2,400 m³/s and V = 350 Mm³. Transform both,
and say what the pair tells you that neither number does alone.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

u₁ = F_Q(2400) = **0.9917**, u₂ = F_V(350) = **0.7586**.

The pair says: **an exceptional peak with an unremarkable volume** — roughly a 1-in-120-year peak
delivering only a 1-in-4-year volume. Physically that is a short, intense convective event rather
than a prolonged monsoon spell.

Why it matters: for a **levee** (OR, peak-driven) this is close to a design event. For a
**reservoir** (AND, needs sustained volume) it is nearly harmless. The same year is a crisis or a
non-event depending on the structure — which is D2, arriving as data rather than as a principle.
</details>

**I3.** Day 1 said a Normal margin understates extremes; Day 2 says a Gaussian copula understates
joint extremes. Are these the same error? Argue both sides, then decide.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**The case that they are the same:** both come from the Gaussian family's exponential-square decay;
both are invisible in the body and unbounded in the tail; both are anti-conservative; both pass the
diagnostics you can compute and fail where you must extrapolate (E3).

**The case that they are different:** they are mathematically independent failures. Sklar (C1)
guarantees you can pair a Gaussian *copula* with heavy-tailed GEV *margins*, or a Gaussian margin
with a Gumbel copula. Fixing one does nothing whatever for the other, and a paper that fixes only
the margins has not addressed the joint problem at all.

**Decide:** they are the *same error made twice*, in two places that must be repaired separately.
The shared intuition is why you should be suspicious of both; Sklar is why suspicion of one is not
evidence about the other. Treating them as one problem is how a project ends up with beautifully
fitted GEV margins and a Gaussian copula quietly zeroing out the compounding.
</details>

**I4.** You have 40 years of peak and volume. Someone asks for the 1,000-year AND design event.
What can you honestly deliver?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Honestly: **a number with a stated family assumption and an interval, plus an explicit statement
that it is an extrapolation of 25× the record length.** Concretely —

- The **dependence** is estimable: τ̂ from 40 pairs has a standard error near 0.1, which is
  workable.
- The **tail dependence** is not (E4): 2 usable points at u = 0.95, none at 0.99. Family choice
  rests on physical argument, not on this record.
- The **margins** beyond u = 0.976 are extrapolation (G5), worth 1.58× on the design value.
- Therefore the deliverable is a **table**: design pairs under 2–3 copula families × 2 margin
  choices, with the spread shown, and the recommendation argued physically.

What you cannot honestly deliver is a single pair of numbers. The request assumes a precision that
40 years does not contain, and the useful response is to say so and hand back the range — not to
refuse, and not to pick the middle.
</details>

**I5.** Show that if C = Π then λ_U = 0, and interpret.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

With C(u,u) = u², apply E2:

$$\frac{1-2u+u^2}{1-u} = \frac{(1-u)^2}{1-u} = 1-u \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \text{ as } u \to 1$$

**Interpretation:** under independence the conditional probability equals the marginal, exactly as
A4(b) requires — P(U₂ > u | U₁ > u) = 1 − u = P(U₂ > u) at *every* cutoff, not just in the limit.
The curve is not merely heading to zero; it never deviated from the marginal in the first place.

The instructive comparison is with the Gaussian, which also has λ_U = 0 but is *not* independent at
any finite u (0.474 against 0.100 at u = 0.90). **Asymptotic independence is not independence.**
The Gaussian copula spends the entire observable range looking dependent and only surrenders its
dependence in a limit no dataset can reach — which is exactly what makes it dangerous rather than
merely wrong.
</details>

**I6.** In one paragraph, connect Day 2 to the project's next methodological step. Write it as if
for your research log.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

A model answer — yours should differ in wording, not in structure:

> Day 2 supplies the vocabulary the copula workflow is actually written in: the marginal fits are
> marginal probabilities, u_i = F_i(x_i) is the probability integral transform, the copula is the
> joint, and λ_U is a conditional probability evaluated in the tail. Sklar's theorem is what makes
> the margin question and the dependence question separable, which is the licence for fitting
> semiparametrically on empirical ranks — margin misspecification then cannot contaminate θ̂,
> though it still governs the design value on the way back out. The immediate next step is
> λ̂_U(u) over u ∈ [0.9, 0.99] against Gumbel, Gaussian and Clayton candidates, and Day 2's
> uncomfortable finding is that on 40 years those diagnostics carry a standard error larger than
> the gap they are meant to resolve. So family selection has to be argued physically and reported
> as a spread across families rather than settled empirically — which changes the shape of the
> results section, not just its numbers.
</details>
