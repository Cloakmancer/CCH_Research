Why the bell curve under-predicts floods

Five things to play with. Each one takes the same idea a step further: real river data is right-skewed, a Normal distribution is not, and the gap between them is almost zero in the middle and enormous in the tail — which is exactly where a cascading hazard is decided. In words: a handful of monster years drag the mean upward. The median barely moves, because it only cares about which value sits in the middle when you line them up — one enormous flood moves the middle by one place, not by a thousand cubic metres. So mode < median < mean is not a formula to memorise; it is just the arithmetic of a long right tail. And it means that saying "average discharge is 800" describes a year that is actually — the mean is not typical.

In words: the Normal curve's tail dies off as e−z²/2 — squared in the exponent, which is savagely fast. Three standard deviations out it already says "1 in 740"; four says "1 in 31,600"; five says "basically never". Real skewed data does not fall away anything like that quickly. So the further into the tail you go, the worse the Normal gets — and it gets worse fast, not gradually. Notice the error number above growing as you drag right. That single behaviour is the entire reason this matters.

And why it matters here specifically: a compound cascading hazard is defined by thresholds. The reservoir overtops, then the slope saturates, then it fails, then the debris dams the channel. Every link in that chain is a "does it cross q*?" question. So a CCH analysis spends its entire life in the part of the distribution where the Normal assumption is most wrong. The error is not small and spread out; it is large and concentrated precisely on the number you are trying to compute.

4The rank transform — how copulas dodge this problem

Here is the trick that makes the copula machinery work. Take those annual maxima, throw away the actual numbers, and keep only each value's rank: smallest gets 1, largest gets n, then divide by n+1. Press the button and watch the skew evaporate.

ui = R(xi) / (n + 1)

What just happened. Before: everything piles up on the left with a few stragglers trailing right. After: the points are spread evenly between 0 and 1 — perfectly uniform, by construction, no matter how skewed the original was. The shape problem is gone.

Why the (n+1) and not n. Divide by n and the largest value maps to exactly 1.0 — the very top of the probability scale, i.e. "this is the largest flood physically possible". You do not know that. The n+1 keeps every point strictly inside (0,1) and leaves room above the largest observation for floods you simply haven't seen yet.

But read the small print: the rank transform only solves half the problem. It strips the marginal shape away so the copula can study the dependence between two variables cleanly. The moment you convert back to real units to state a design discharge in m³/s, you need the marginal distribution again — GEV, log-Pearson III, log-normal, whichever — and if you pick it wrong, your design number is wrong even with a flawless copula. Two separate problems, kept separate. That is exactly why this project fits copulas semiparametrically: use the empirical ranks for the margins, so that a bad guess at the margin cannot contaminate the dependence parameter θ̂.

5The same mistake, one dimension up

This is the payoff. A flood has two properties that matter — its peak and its volume — and a cascade usually needs both to be extreme at once. In words: the Gaussian copula has zero upper-tail dependence. That phrase means something very concrete and very strange: the further into the extremes you look, the more it insists the two drivers become independent. Its number falls — 0.57, 0.47, 0.39, 0.32 — and keeps falling toward zero. Gumbel's sits flat at about 0.59 no matter how far out you push, because for this family the limit is a fixed constant, λU = 2 − 21/θ = 2 − √2. It genuinely believes that an extreme peak brings an extreme volume with it. And it should: peak and volume come from the same storm. They ought to get more tightly bound at the extremes, not less. That is the argument for Gumbel as the lead family — not an empirical preference, but the same structural error as Panel 2, one dimension up.
