# Day 2 — Joint, marginal and conditional probability, and the road to a copula

*2026-07-29*

## Concept

Marginal, joint and conditional probability read off a discrete contingency table; the
multiplication rule $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B|A)$; independence as the special case in which
$P(B|A) = P(B)$; Bayes' theorem as the same rule solved for the reverse conditional; and the
probability integral transform, $U = F_X(X) \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ for any continuous $X$
whatever its own shape.

The standing example is 500 monsoon days cross-classified by heavy rainfall $R$ and high antecedent
soil saturation $S$: 32 days with both, 18 rain-only, 118 saturation-only, 332 neither.

![Two mosaic plots of the same 500 days. The observed panel puts 32 in the both-cell; independence requires 15. The column widths, which are the margins, are identical in the two panels](images/day2-contingency.png)

*The margins are the same in both panels and carry no information about the association. Only the
interior moves.*

| | Read as | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal $P(R)$ | 50/500 | 0.10 |
| Marginal $P(S)$ | 150/500 | 0.30 |
| Joint $P(R \cap S)$ | 32/500 | 0.064 |
| Conditional $P(S \mid R)$ | 32/50 | 0.640 |
| Conditional $P(R \mid S)$ | 32/150 | 0.213 |

Independence would require $P(R)P(S) = 0.030$, i.e. 15 days rather than 32 — a lift of 2.13, an
odds ratio of 5.00, and $\chi^2 = 30.6$ on one degree of freedom.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Where "lift 2.13, odds ratio 5.00, χ² = 30.6" come from</b></summary>

Three ways of saying the one thing — the observed *both*-cell (32 days) is bigger than the 15 that
independence predicts — each scaled differently.

**Lift** is the plain ratio of the observed joint probability to the one independence would give:

$$\text{lift} = \frac{P(R \cap S)}{P(R)\,P(S)} = \frac{0.064}{0.10 \times 0.30} = \frac{0.064}{0.030} = 2.13.$$

"Rain and saturation land together 2.13 times as often as chance alone would produce."

**Odds ratio** compares the *odds* of saturation on rainy days against dry days. With the four
counts laid out — 32 both, 18 rain-only, 118 saturation-only, 332 neither — it is just the
cross-product of the table:

$$\text{OR} = \frac{32 \times 332}{18 \times 118} = \frac{10\,624}{2\,124} = 5.00.$$

Unlike lift, it does not shift when a whole row or column is rescaled, which is why it is the
measure epidemiology reaches for.

**χ²** measures the whole table's distance from independence at once. Independence predicts
15, 35, 135, 315 in the four cells; because a table with fixed margins has only one free number,
every cell is off by the *same* amount, ±17. So

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O-E)^2}{E} = \frac{17^2}{15} + \frac{17^2}{35} + \frac{17^2}{135} + \frac{17^2}{315} = 30.6.$$

On one degree of freedom anything past about 3.84 is significant at 5%, so 30.6 says plainly: this
association is not sampling noise.
</details>

## What it actually says

Three things that the elementary presentation tends to leave implicit, and that the CCH bridge
needs explicitly.

**All three probabilities differ only in their denominator.** Marginal and joint both divide by the
whole sample; a conditional divides by a subset. The 32 days appear in the numerator of the joint
*and* of both conditionals — 0.064, 0.640 and 0.213 are the same days measured against three
different worlds. Nearly every probability error in an applied paper is a denominator that quietly
changed, and $P(\text{landslide} \mid \text{extreme rain}) \ne P(\text{extreme rain} \mid
\text{landslide})$ is the instance that will matter here.

**The margins fix almost nothing.** With $P(R) = 0.10$ and $P(S) = 0.30$ held fixed, the four cells
have two margin constraints plus a total, leaving exactly **one free number**; and that number is
confined to $[\max(0, 0.10+0.30-1),\ \min(0.10,\,0.30)] = [0,\ 0.10]$. Independence sits at 0.030
and the observation at 0.064, 64% of the way up the range.

![Four straight lines, one per table cell, plotted against the free number p. Each line is a cell probability; the shaded band marks the only stretch of p where all four stay on or above zero, which is the interval from 0 to 0.10](images/day2-frechet-construction.png)

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>How those two bounds are built — step by step</b></summary>

Start with why there is only *one* number to argue about at all. The table has four cells, so four
unknowns. Fixing $P(R) = 0.10$ ties the two rain cells together — they must total 50 days; fixing
$P(S) = 0.30$ ties the two saturated cells together — 150 days; and all four cells together must sum
to the whole 500. That is three equations on four unknowns, so exactly one cell is free to choose
and the other three follow by subtraction. Take the free one to be the "both" corner,
$p = P(R \cap S)$:

| cell | forced to equal | value |
|---|---|---|
| both | your one free choice | $p$ |
| rain-only | rainy days left once the both-days are removed | $P(R) - p = 0.10 - p$ |
| sat-only | saturated days left over | $P(S) - p = 0.30 - p$ |
| neither | whatever remains of the 500 | $1 - P(R) - P(S) + p = 0.60 + p$ |

Now the whole constraint, and there is only one: a cell counts days, so it cannot be negative.
Reading it off each row, both $\ge 0$ forces $p \ge 0$; rain-only $\ge 0$ forces $p \le 0.10$;
sat-only $\ge 0$ forces $p \le 0.30$; neither $\ge 0$ forces $p \ge -0.60$. All four must hold at
once. The two upper limits collapse to the tighter one, $p \le \min(0.10,\,0.30) = 0.10$; the two
lower limits collapse to the tighter one, $p \ge \max(0,\,-0.60) = 0$. So $p$ is trapped in

$$\big[\max(0,\; P(R)+P(S)-1),\ \ \min(P(R),\,P(S))\big] = [0,\ 0.10],$$

exactly the interval the passage asserts. The figure above is this argument drawn out: each cell is
a straight line in $p$, and the shaded band is the only stretch where all four lines sit on or above
the axis. Two constraints do the clipping — the both-line rising off zero on the left, the rain-only
line reaching zero on the right — while the sat-only and neither constraints never bite, which is
the entire reason the formula carries a $\min$ and a $\max$ rather than one term each.

What makes the two endpoints worth naming is that each is a recognisable *kind of dependence*, not
just an arithmetic stop. At the top, $p = 0.10$ drives rain-only to zero: every rainy day is also
saturated, so the smaller event $R$ sits wholly inside $S$ and $P(S \mid R) = 1$. That is the
strongest positive association these margins permit. At the bottom, $p = 0$ drives the both-cell to
zero: rain and saturation never share a day — mutual exclusion, the strongest *negative* association
possible here. Independence, at 0.030, is neither extreme; it is the by-chance value $P(R)\,P(S)$
from the earlier lesson, sitting between them. The observation, 0.064, lands 64% of the way from the
mutually-exclusive floor toward the fully-nested ceiling — real positive dependence, but well short
of the most the margins would allow.

That is what the note is driving at. These two endpoints are the **Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds**, and
in copula language they are the most positively and most negatively dependent structures *any* pair
of margins can be glued into. The $2\times2$ table is that statement shrunk until all of it is
visible at once, and the single fraction "64% of the way up" — where the joint sits inside its own
admissible interval — is the one thing a copula reports, with whatever the margins happened to be
already divided out.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Check yourself</b></summary>

Suppose the margins were instead $P(R) = 0.20$ and $P(S) = 0.50$. Before reading on: what interval
is $P(R \cap S)$ confined to, and where would independence fall inside it?

The bounds are $\big[\max(0,\,0.20+0.50-1),\ \min(0.20,\,0.50)\big] = [\max(0,-0.30),\ 0.20] =
[0,\ 0.20]$, and independence sits at $0.20 \times 0.50 = 0.10$, exactly halfway up. The floor is
still 0 because $0.20 + 0.50 = 0.70 < 1$: the two events are small enough to miss each other
entirely. Push the margins past 1 — say $P(R) = 0.60$, $P(S) = 0.70$ — and the floor lifts to
$0.60 + 0.70 - 1 = 0.30$: now they are too big to avoid, and some co-occurrence is forced no matter
how you arrange the table.
</details>
</details>

![The admissible range for P(R and S) drawn as a line from 0.000 to 0.100, with independence at 0.030 and the observed value at 0.064](images/day2-frechet.png)

Those two bounds are the **Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds** $W$ and $M$, and they hold in exactly this
form for continuous copulas. A $2\times2$ table with fixed margins *is* a copula, in the only case
small enough to see all at once — one number saying where in the admissible interval the joint
sits, in a form that does not depend on what the margins happened to be.

**Bayes is a rearrangement, not a second law.** The joint factors two ways, $P(R)P(S|R) =
P(R \cap S) = P(S)P(R|S)$, because "rain then saturation" and "saturation then rain" describe the
same 32 days. Dividing the outer equality by $P(S)$ *is* Bayes' theorem. There is nothing about
belief in the derivation.

Then the step that carries all of this into the continuous case. For continuous, strictly
increasing $F$,

$$P(F(X) \le u) = P\!\left(X \le F^{-1}(u)\right) = F\!\left(F^{-1}(u)\right) = u$$

so $U = F(X)$ is exactly Uniform(0,1) — whatever $X$ was. The condition is continuity, and it is
not free: daily rainfall has an atom at zero, so the transform is *not* exactly uniform on a raw
daily series, which is a live decision for the Nepal data in `04_Resources/` rather than a
technicality.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Why each equals sign in that line holds</b></summary>

Read $U = F(X)$ as "replace every value by its own percentile," and the claim is that those
percentiles come out perfectly evenly spread between 0 and 1. The proof is one honest chain, taken a
link at a time for some cutoff $u$ between 0 and 1. It opens with the question itself,
$P(F(X) \le u)$ — how often is the percentile at most $u$? Because $F$ is strictly increasing it has
an inverse, and applying $F^{-1}$ to both sides of $F(X) \le u$ leaves the inequality pointing the
same way, so this is the same event as $P(X \le F^{-1}(u))$ — "value at most the $u$-th quantile."
That is now a plain CDF evaluation: by the definition $P(X \le a) = F(a)$ with $a = F^{-1}(u)$, it
equals $F(F^{-1}(u))$, and a function cancels its own inverse to leave $u$. So $P(U \le u) = u$ for
every $u$, and a CDF that runs as a straight line from 0 to 1 *is* the definition of
$\text{Uniform}(0,1)$. The shape of $X$ never entered the argument — only that $F$ is continuous and
increasing — which is exactly why any continuous variable, however lopsided, flattens to the same
uniform.

The catch lives in the second step, where the inverse was used. If $F$ has a jump — daily rainfall
stacks a lump of probability precisely at 0 mm — then a whole range of $u$ values maps to that one
point, and $F(X)$ skips the interval under the jump instead of filling it. The transform is then
only approximately uniform, and the size of the gap is the size of the atom. For the Nepal daily
series that is a modelling choice to make on purpose, not a rounding error to ignore.
</details>

## Bridge to CCH

CCH hazard variables — rainfall, discharge, soil saturation — each carry a different marginal
distribution, fitted independently per variable, the same way a marginal probability in the table
is read off one row or column at a time. Before they can be modelled jointly, each is passed
through its own fitted or empirical CDF, $u_i = F_i(x_i)$. That is the probability integral
transform applied variable-by-variable, and it is the entire content of *"map marginal CDFs to
copula input structure"*: every hazard, regardless of native shape or units, is re-expressed on a
common percentile scale before any dependence model is fitted. Four consequences follow.

**1. Sklar's theorem is what makes the two hard problems separable.** The joint model is
$F(x_1,\dots,x_n) = C(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_n(x_n))$: a copula $C$ fitted to the transformed
$(U_1,\dots,U_n)$, chosen independently of the margins. This is the joint-probability concept from
the table, generalised past two discrete categories to continuous, differently-shaped variables
sharing one dependence structure.

![Three scatter plots: the same 1,200 points on the unit square in the middle, mapped through two different pairs of margins on either side; the middle panel is identical in both cases](images/day2-sklar.png)

Change the margins and the physical scatter distorts beyond recognition while the unit-square
scatter does not move a single point — $F$ is monotone, so it cannot reorder anything, and the
copula is nothing but the ranks. That invariance is the licence for arguing about GEV-versus-LP3
and about Gumbel-versus-Gaussian as **two separate arguments** rather than one tangled one.

**2. The quantity that decides a cascade is a conditional probability.** Upper-tail dependence,
$\lambda_U = \lim_{u \to 1} P(U_2 > u \mid U_1 > u)$, is literally $P(B|A)$ evaluated in the extreme
corner: given one hazard is at an extreme percentile, how likely is a second to be extreme
simultaneously. A high $\lambda_U$ means the hazards compound; $\lambda_U$ near zero means they
behave close to independently at extremes, and treating them as compounding would overstate risk.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>What that limit means, and where 0.586 comes from</b></summary>

Strip away the limit notation and $\lambda_U$ is the same conditional probability as $P(S \mid R)$
from the table, only asked at the very edge of the data. Fix a high percentile $u$, say 0.99, and
keep only the days where hazard 1 cleared it — the top 1% of peaks. Of those, what fraction also
have hazard 2 in its own top 1%? That fraction is $P(U_2 > u \mid U_1 > u)$, an ordinary conditional
with a shrinking denominator. Now push $u$ toward 1 — top 0.5%, top 0.1%, top 0.01% — and watch
where the fraction settles. If it settles on a positive number the two hazards keep arriving
together however far into the tail you look, and that limiting number is $\lambda_U$; if it drains
to zero they come apart in the extremes and behave independently in exactly the corner that would
hurt most.

For the Gumbel copula the limit is not read off a chart, it is a closed form. Its single parameter
$\theta$ is pinned by Kendall's $\tau$ through $\theta = 1/(1-\tau)$, so $\tau = 0.5$ gives
$\theta = 2$, and then $\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta} = 2 - 2^{1/2} = 2 - 1.414 = 0.586$. The 0.589
quoted in the text is the same quantity measured at the finite cutoff $u = 0.99$, still drifting
down toward its limit of 0.586. The Gaussian copula fitted to the *same* $\tau = 0.5$ has
$\lambda_U = 0$ exactly — its curve is bound for zero, it simply has not arrived by $u = 0.99$. Same
rank correlation, opposite verdict on the corner, which is the entire warning of the panel.
</details>

![Four conditional-probability curves against the quantile cutoff: Gumbel flattening onto 0.586 while Gaussian, Clayton and independence all decline toward zero](images/day2-tail-dependence-families.png)

All four families above are fitted to the **same** Kendall's $\tau = 0.5$. At $u = 0.99$ they give
0.589, 0.274, 0.029 and 0.010 — a factor of 59 between the widest pair, on data that agrees exactly
on every summary statistic a reader is likely to be shown. Clayton is the instructive one: it has
*more* tail dependence than Gumbel, $\lambda_L = 0.707$, but in the **lower** corner, where two
variables being simultaneously small is what binds. For a drought study it is the right family; for
a flood cascade it is the worst of the four. "Has tail dependence" is half a property — always ask
which tail.

**3. The error is exponential in the length of the chain.** Three links, each requiring $u > 0.99$:

![Three chained boxes — rainfall, discharge, slope failure — giving one in 291 years when chained by tail dependence against one in a million under independence](images/day2-cascade-chain.png)

Independence gives $0.01^3 = 10^{-6}$, one in a million years. Chaining through $\lambda_U = 0.586$
gives $0.01 \times 0.586^2 = 3.43 \times 10^{-3}$, **one in 291**. Each additional link multiplies
the discrepancy by another $\lambda_U/(1-u) \approx 58.6$: two links wrong by 59×, three by 3,431×,
four by roughly 200,000×.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>The chain arithmetic, link by link</b></summary>

One "link" is a single hazard clearing the 99th percentile, probability $1 - u = 0.01$ on its own.
The whole disagreement is about what happens to the *second* and *third* links once the first has
already fired. Independence treats each link as ignorant of the others and just multiplies the raw
0.01s, so three links give $0.01^3 = 10^{-6}$, one in a million years. Tail dependence says that once
the first hazard is extreme the next is extreme with probability $\lambda_U = 0.586$, not 0.01 — so
the first link still costs its 0.01 to trigger the chain, and each further link multiplies by 0.586:
$0.01 \times 0.586 \times 0.586 = 3.43 \times 10^{-3}$, one in 291.

The ratio between the two answers is what matters, and it grows by a fixed factor per link, because
each extra link costs 0.01 under independence but 0.586 under dependence — a per-link ratio of
$0.586 / 0.01 = 58.6$:

| chain length | independence | tail-dependent | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 link | $10^{-2}$ | $10^{-2}$ | 1× |
| 2 links | $10^{-4}$ | $5.9 \times 10^{-3}$ | 59× |
| 3 links | $10^{-6}$ | $3.4 \times 10^{-3}$ | 3,400× |
| 4 links | $10^{-8}$ | $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | 201,000× |

The independence column falls off a cliff — each link divides it by 100 — while the dependent column
barely moves, each link merely multiplying by 0.586. The gap between them is 58.6 raised to the
number of links, which is how a two-hazard discrepancy you could almost live with becomes a
four-hazard discrepancy of 200,000×. And it does not err on the safe side: the naive arithmetic
makes a lethal cascade read as a non-event.
</details>

This is the sharpest available statement of why the copula machinery is not decoration on this
project. A single-hazard analysis that wrongly assumes independence is wrong by a *bounded* factor.
A **cascading** analysis is wrong by that factor raised to the length of the chain — and cascades
are the object of study.

**4. Margin choice and dependence remain separate problems, and both stay live.** The bridge note
supplied for this session put it as: an incorrectly light-tailed marginal *"will understate λ and
understate compounding risk."* The conclusion is right and the mechanism needs sharpening, because
the sharpened version is more useful.

![Left: the same u = 0.99 read back through four candidate margins gives design values from 1,824 to 2,876 m³/s. Right: lambda_U is 0.586 under all four](images/day2-margin-vs-copula.png)

$\lambda_U$ is a property of the **copula alone** and is invariant to the margins — that invariance
*is* Sklar's theorem. Changing the marginal family cannot move the true $\lambda_U$; the figure
shows four candidate margins for peak discharge putting the $u = 0.99$ design value anywhere from
1,824 to 2,876 m³/s, a spread of 1.58×, while $\lambda_U$ sits at 0.5858 in every one. What is true
is two separate things:

- **The estimate is contaminated if the margins are parametric.** Under IFM the pseudo-observations
  are $\hat{F}_i(x_i)$; if $\hat{F}_i$ is misspecified they are not uniform, and $\hat\lambda_U$
  absorbs a marginal error as though it were dependence. This is exactly the argument for
  semiparametric fitting on empirical ranks, already the project's adopted default
  ([Research Log, 2026-07-26](research-log.md)).
- **The design value depends on the margin regardless.** Getting $\lambda_U$ right buys a correct
  *percentile*; converting that percentile back to m³/s re-invokes the margin, and an empirical CDF
  has no data beyond the record — for 40 years it maxes out at $u = 40/41 = 0.976$, so a
  $u = 0.99$ design value is extrapolation whatever the copula did.

Worth fixing in the write-up: a reviewer who knows copulas will read "misspecified margins change
$\lambda_U$" as a misunderstanding of Sklar, and the argument does not need the overclaim.

## Feynman check — the whole thing without the notation

*If you cannot explain it plainly you have not understood it.*

Start with something completely ordinary. Take 500 days at a river and sort them into four piles:
heavy rain and wet ground, heavy rain and dry ground, no rain and wet ground, neither. Count each
pile. Every question you can ask about how rain and wet ground go together is now just a matter of
choosing what to divide by. Divide by all 500 and you learn how often each thing happens on its
own. Divide one pile by 500 and you learn how often they happen together. Divide that same pile by
only the rainy days, and you learn how often the ground is already wet **when** it rains hard — 64%,
against 30% on an average day. Same 32 days each time. Different question each time, because
different denominator.

Now the part that surprises people. The row and column totals — 50 rainy days, 150 wet-ground days
— tell you *nothing* about whether the two travel together. Once those totals are fixed there is
exactly one number left to choose, and it can be anything from 0 to 50 days. That single leftover
number is the whole relationship. Everything else about the table was decided before you looked.

That leftover number is what a copula is. The only difficulty in going from a table of counts to
real hazard data is that rainfall and discharge do not come in four tidy piles — they come as
continuous quantities with wildly different shapes and units, one measured in millimetres, one in
cubic metres per second, one as a bare fraction between zero and one. The trick that fixes this is
almost too simple to feel like a trick: stop reporting the value and report the **percentile**
instead. Not "1,800 cubic metres per second" but "wetter than 96.7% of years". Do that to every
variable and they all come out on the same scale — perfectly evenly spread between 0 and 1, no
matter how lopsided they started. Different units, different shapes, one common language. And it
throws away nothing about how the variables move together, because a percentile is just a ranking,
and rankings are exactly what "do these two arrive at the same time?" is about.

What it *does* throw away is size. After the transform, the worst flood on record is merely "the
biggest one" — one notch above the second biggest, and the data no longer knows whether it was
twice the usual or twenty times. So you get to answer "how often do two hazards go extreme
together?" cleanly, and you have to put the real units back before you can say how big the
resulting event is. Two problems, deliberately kept apart, and both still have to be solved.

Then the number the whole project turns on, which is not a new idea but the old one asked in a new
place. Instead of "how often is the ground wet when it rains hard", ask "how often is the flood
volume extreme **when the peak is extreme**" — and then keep pushing what "extreme" means further
out. Top 10%, top 5%, top 1%, top 0.1%. Some models say that answer settles down and stays put:
push as far as you like and roughly 59% of extreme peaks still bring an extreme volume with them.
Other models say it drains away to nothing — that the further out you look, the more the two
hazards behave as though they had never met. Those are not slightly different forecasts. They are
opposite claims about the physics, and the standard summary number everyone reports is **identical**
for both of them. You cannot tell them apart from the summary. You have to look at the corner.

And here is the sting. Chain three of these together — the rain is extreme, so the river is
extreme, so the slope fails — and multiply the probabilities as if none of them knew about the
others, and you get one such disaster every million years. Chain them the way models with real tail
dependence say you should, and you get one every 291 years. Same data. Same everything. A factor of
three and a half thousand, and it points the wrong way: the naive arithmetic makes the structure
look safe. Add a fourth link and the gap grows to two hundred thousand. That is why a *cascading*
hazard needs this machinery when a single hazard often does not — the mistake compounds exactly as
fast as the hazard does.

## Open thread

* **The corner cannot be measured on 40 years.** Estimating $\lambda_U$ at $u = 0.95$ uses only the
  $n(1-u) = 2$ observations above the cutoff, giving a binomial standard error of $\pm 0.35$ against
  a Gumbel-vs-Gaussian gap of 0.20 — the error bar is 1.7× the thing it must resolve. At $u = 0.99$
  a 40-year record contains 0.4 expected points, i.e. usually none.

  ![Gumbel and Gaussian conditional-probability curves with binomial error bars at u = 0.90 and 0.95 for a 40-year record; the error bars dwarf the gap between the families](images/day2-record-resolution.png)

  It takes roughly 1,000 years of annual maxima to get the $u = 0.99$ standard error down to
  $\pm 0.156$, still comparable to the 0.315 gap. So family selection **cannot be settled
  empirically from one station**. It has to lean on physical argument (peak and volume come out of
  the same storm), on regional pooling to buy effective record length, or on carrying 2–3 families
  through to design values and reporting the spread. This is a scope limit on the results section,
  not just a caveat — worth deciding before the analysis is written, not after.

* **Ties and atoms.** Daily rainfall has a point mass at zero, so $U = F(X)$ is not exactly uniform
  on a raw daily series and rank-based estimators see ties. Mixed distribution, wet-day-conditional
  amounts, or jittering — the choice has to be made explicitly for the Nepal series and recorded.

* **Which return period.** AND, OR and Kendall are three different questions, not three estimates
  of one. At $u = 0.90$ under Gumbel the AND period is 16.2 years and the OR period 7.2. The
  project already has an [AND/OR decision tool](interactive/and-or-decision.html){target=_blank};
  what it does not yet have is a decision *recorded* for each structure type in the study area.
