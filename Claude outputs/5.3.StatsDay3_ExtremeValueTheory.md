## Concept

Taught from zero — first exposure to both Maximum Likelihood Estimation and extreme value theory,
started with a calibration check: MLE background "never done it," EVT/GEV background "completely
new." Everything below built up from that starting point, in the order it actually happened.

**Opening prequestion.** What does fitting a GEV to a daily-rainfall series with an atom at zero
require, beyond a standard continuous-data MLE fit? Answer: "i don't knowww teach this to me" —
this is what triggered rebuilding the whole thing from first principles rather than answering it
directly.

**Check 1 — MLE via coin flips.** Asked why MLE picks p=0.8 for 8 heads out of 10 flips, rather
than 0.5. **Correct**, and in his own words the reasoning was: MLE is calculating, from the set of
data actually observed, how likely that data is to occur — the most probable probability of
occurrence given the data. So it picks 0.8 rather than 0.5 because, for that specific outcome
(8 heads, 2 tails), 0.8 is the value under which that outcome is most likely. He then extended it
himself, unprompted: with 100 samples, 40 heads and 60 tails, MLE would give 0.4 "without knowing
anything else" — correctly generalizing the rule to a new case before being asked to.

**MLE for continuous data — the worked example that followed.** Since coin flips only have
"probabilities," not densities, the next step was showing how MLE works when data is continuous
(rainfall depths, not heads/tails) — no single value has a probability, only a density: a curve,
tall where data is plausible, low where it isn't. Fit Normal($\mu$, $\sigma=1$) to $\{2, 4, 6\}$:

$$f(x;\mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2}\right)$$

At $\mu=4$: heights are 0.0540, 0.3989, 0.0540 → product (likelihood) = **0.001163**.
At $\mu=3$: heights are 0.2420, 0.2420, 0.00443 → product = **0.000260**.

$\mu=4$ (the sample mean) wins — about 4.5× more likely than $\mu=3$ — and calculus (derivative of
the log-likelihood, set to zero) proves the sample mean is the exact maximiser over *all* candidate
$\mu$, not just a better guess than 3. This is exactly what `scipy.stats.<dist>.fit()` does
numerically instead of by hand — same search.

**Check 2 — why multiply the heights instead of adding them.** His answer: in the case of a sum, a
larger value can offset a smaller one for the same candidate, but the product is much less
forgiving — so for the likelihood to matter, all values in the candidate set need to perform well.
**Partially correct** — a real and useful observation about the *behaviour* of the product, but not
the underlying reason it's a product in the first place. Correction: it's forced by independence,
$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\cdot P(B)$ for independent draws — that's the only way to get a valid "joint
probability of this whole dataset" out of the individual densities at all. The "unforgiving"
behaviour he noticed is a real consequence of that rule, not the reason for choosing it.

**Why GEV needs its own theorem.** Fitting a Normal (or any "typical values" distribution) to
describe rare extremes means extrapolating a model into a region it was never built to fit. The
Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem is the fix — structurally the same *shape* of result as the Central
Limit Theorem, but about a different summary statistic: CLT says the **average** of many values
converges to Normal, regardless of the parent distribution. FTG says the **maximum** of many values
converges to GEV, regardless of the parent distribution.

**Check 3 — why not fit GEV directly to raw daily data.** His answer: "because daily sample
averages out giving us the normal distribution as per the central limit theorem." **Incorrect — a
genuine misconception**, and the one worth remembering. Nothing is being averaged in a raw daily
reading — each day's rainfall is a single measurement, not a sum or average of many things — so CLT
doesn't apply to it in either direction. The real reason: FTG is a claim about maxima specifically.
Raw daily data is "block size 1" — not a maximum of anything — so fitting GEV to it directly isn't
imprecise, it's outside what the theorem promises at all, a category error. The fix: chop the record
into blocks (one year is standard), keep only each block's maximum, and fit GEV to *that* derived
series. This is also why a "100-year flood" — a return-period statement read off a fitted GEV curve
— isn't obtainable from a Normal fit to daily values.

**Check 4 — monthly vs. annual block size.** Asked what would happen to fit quality with monthly
maxima (block ≈30) instead of annual (block ≈365). His answer: "a poorer fit as it is the max of
less amount of data." **Correct as far as it went** — bigger blocks sit closer to the FTG limit, so
monthly blocks carry more approximation bias. What was added: the other half of the tradeoff he
didn't mention — monthly blocks give far more data points (≈492 vs. 41 for the same 41-year record),
a real bias/variance tradeoff, and part of why peaks-over-threshold/GPD and r-largest-order-statistic
methods exist in the EVT literature at all — ways to extract more than one extreme value per year
without abandoning the theory.

**Atom at zero — and the twist connecting back to block size.** A continuous density can make a
curve tall near a value, but it can never assign a real lump of probability to one *exact* point —
that needs a point mass ("atom"). Whether something is a genuine atom depends on whether the
data-generating process can structurally repeat that exact value with real, nonzero probability —
not on how many times it happens to show up in one sample. This connects directly back to block
size: monthly blocks can reintroduce a real (if small) atom at zero, because a fully dry month is
genuinely possible in the dry season (Nov–Feb) — unlike a fully dry *year*, which essentially never
happens in a monsoon climate.

**Check 5 — does a single zero among monthly maxima count as an atom.** First pass: "i donot
understand the question" — question rephrased to be concrete. Second pass: "i dont know." Answered
directly rather than pushed further, since two passes hadn't landed it: atom-ness is about whether
the *generating process* has a real, repeatable chance of producing exactly zero, not about how many
zeros happen to appear in one particular sample. Daily: huge atom (65–80% of all days across the
280-station Nepal record, exact 0.0 every time it doesn't rain). Monthly: small but real atom (a
bone-dry month is rare but genuinely possible). Annual, monsoon Nepal: no atom at all (a bone-dry
year isn't a realistic outcome).

## What it actually says

GEV is fit to block maxima, not raw daily data, so the daily zero-atom never reaches the fit — as
long as the block is large enough that a zero-block is a genuine impossibility. Annual blocks
satisfy that in Nepal; monthly blocks would not. This is why the actual fit (2026-08-04 session,
`data/Daily Rainfall all nepal.xlsx`, logged in `1.ProjectStatud.md`) used plain
`scipy.stats.genextreme.fit()` on annual maxima with no zero-inflation treatment, and why that was
the *correct* call rather than a shortcut — the mixture/hurdle-likelihood machinery the opening
question pointed at is real, just one level upstream: needed only if fitting a distribution to the
whole daily series (e.g. the $U = F(X)$ transform for copula pseudo-observations), not to extracted
block maxima.

## Bridge to CCH

**1. This is a real margin for the copula workflow, not a toy example.** Fitted results, 5 stations
with 100%-complete 1980–2020 daily records:

| Station | years | ξ | μ | σ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kathmandu Airport | 41 | −0.003 | 74.1 | 17.3 |
| Pokhara Airport | 41 | −0.061 | 173.1 | 38.7 |
| Simara Airport | 41 | −0.101 | 147.4 | 41.1 |
| Biratnagar Airport | 41 | −0.185 | 118.3 | 38.7 |
| Surkhet | 41 | 0.398 | 96.1 | 25.4 |

This resolves the 2026-07-25 real-data-vs-pseudo-observations question in favour of real data, and
the Day 1 open thread (fit GEV to Nepal rainfall for ξ). It does **not** by itself resolve the
"need a real flood peak-volume dataset" blocker — rainfall AMS is a station-level univariate margin,
not the peak/volume pair the copula stage needs; that requires a separate rainfall→discharge step.

**2. No single national ξ.** −0.19 to +0.40 across five stations rules out treating ξ as a fixed
national constant — the margin has to stay station-specific, or be pooled deliberately (regional
frequency analysis), consistent with the project's standing "report the spread, don't collapse to
one number" convention (Next Action 7).

**3. Same instability shape as Day 2's λ_U finding, one dimension down.** A parametric bootstrap on
ξ gives a 95% CI of (−0.25, 0.28) at Kathmandu but (0.09, 5.0) at Surkhet — pulled by a single
outlier year (423.1 mm vs. next-highest 281.2 mm). Day 2 showed a 40-year record can't resolve
$\lambda_U$ (a bivariate tail parameter) precisely; this shows the same limitation one level down,
for a univariate shape parameter. L-moments/PWM are flagged as the next thing to try — more stable
than MLE at this record length per the standard EVT literature (Coles 2001) — not yet implemented.
