## Concept

Taught from zero — first exposure to both Maximum Likelihood Estimation and extreme value theory.

**MLE, from a coin flip.** MLE picks the parameter value under which the data actually observed
was the most probable outcome. Flip a coin 10 times, get 8 heads: for each candidate $p$,
"probability of exactly this sequence" is computable; search over $p$, and the maximum sits exactly
at $p = 0.8$, the observed proportion. Generalises directly — 40/100 heads gives $\hat p = 0.4$.

**MLE for continuous data, worked example.** No single value has a "probability" for continuous
data, only a density — a curve, tall where data is plausible, low where it isn't. Fit
Normal($\mu$, $\sigma=1$) to $\{2, 4, 6\}$:

$$f(x;\mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2}\right)$$

At $\mu=4$: heights are 0.0540, 0.3989, 0.0540 → product (likelihood) = **0.001163**.
At $\mu=3$: heights are 0.2420, 0.2420, 0.00443 → product = **0.000260**.

$\mu=4$ (the sample mean) wins — about 4.5× more likely than $\mu=3$, and calculus (derivative of
the log-likelihood, set to zero) proves the sample mean is the exact maximiser over *all* candidate
$\mu$. Multiplying the heights rather than adding them isn't a strictness choice, it's forced by
independence — $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$ for independent draws is the only way to get a valid joint
probability from the individual densities. `scipy.stats.<dist>.fit()` runs this same search
numerically.

**Why GEV needs its own theorem.** Fitting a Normal (or any "typical values" distribution) to
describe rare extremes extrapolates a model into a region it was never built for. The
Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem is the fix — structurally the same shape of result as the Central
Limit Theorem, but for a different summary statistic: CLT says the *average* of many values
converges to Normal regardless of the parent distribution; FTG says the *maximum* of many values
converges to GEV, regardless of the parent distribution. Raw daily rainfall is "block size 1" — a
single measurement, not a maximum of anything and not an average of anything either — so fitting
GEV to it directly is outside what either theorem promises, a category error rather than an
imprecision. The fix: chop the record into blocks (one year is standard), keep only each block's
maximum, and fit GEV to *that* derived series.

**Block size tradeoff.** FTG's guarantee is asymptotic in block size — bigger blocks sit closer to
the true GEV limit. Annual maxima (block ≈365) are closer to that target but give only one value a
year — 41 years of data is 41 numbers, a thin sample with noisy parameter estimates (see Bridge,
below). Monthly maxima (block ≈30) are further from the theoretical target but give ~492 points for
the same 41-year record — a real bias/variance tradeoff, and the reason peaks-over-threshold/GPD and
r-largest-order-statistic methods exist in the EVT literature: ways to get more than one extreme
value per year without abandoning the theory.

**Atom at zero.** A continuous density can make a curve tall near a value, but it can never assign
a discrete lump of real probability to one exact point — that needs a point mass ("atom"). Whether
something is a genuine atom depends on whether the data-generating process can structurally repeat
that exact value with real, nonzero probability, not on how many times it happens to show up in one
sample. A dry day (exact 0.0 mm) is common and expected in the daily series — a large atom (65–80%
of all days, all 280 Nepal stations checked). A fully dry month is rare but genuinely possible in
the dry season (Nov–Feb) — a real, smaller atom. A fully dry *year* is not a realistic outcome in a
monsoon climate at all — no atom.

## What it actually says

GEV is fit to block maxima, not raw daily data, so the daily zero-atom never reaches the fit — as
long as the block is large enough that a zero-block is a genuine impossibility. Annual blocks
satisfy that in Nepal; monthly blocks would not. This is why the actual fit (2026-08-04 session,
`data/Daily Rainfall all nepal.xlsx`, logged in `1.ProjectStatud.md`) used plain
`scipy.stats.genextreme.fit()` on annual maxima with no zero-inflation treatment, and why that was
the *correct* call rather than a shortcut — the mixture/hurdle-likelihood machinery the opening
question pointed at is real, just one level upstream, needed only if fitting a distribution to the
whole daily series (e.g. the $U = F(X)$ transform for copula pseudo-observations) rather than to
extracted block maxima.

**Genuine misconception caught this session, worth flagging for next time:** reaching for the
Central Limit Theorem to explain why GEV needs block maxima rather than raw daily data. CLT governs
*averages*; this is a separate, structurally parallel theorem about *maxima*. Raw daily data isn't
an average of anything, so CLT doesn't apply to it in either direction. Everything else was
reconstructed correctly once walked through, most of it self-corrected without the answer being
given away outright.

## Bridge to CCH

**1. This is a real margin for the copula workflow, not a toy example.** Fitted results, 5 stations
with 100%-complete 1980–2020 daily records:

| Station | years | ξ | μ | σ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kathmandu Airport | 41 | −0.003 | 74.1 | 17.3 |
| Pokhara Airport | 41 | −0.061 | 173.1 | 38.7 |
| Simara Airport | 41 | −0.101 | 147.4 | 41.1 |
| Biratnagar Airport | 41 | −0.185 | 118.3 | 38.7 |
| Surkhet | 41 | 0.398 | 96.1 | 25.4 |

This resolves the 2026-07-25 real-data-vs-pseudo-observations question in favour of real data, and
the Day 1 open thread (fit GEV to Nepal rainfall for ξ). It does **not** by itself resolve the
"need a real flood peak-volume dataset" blocker — rainfall AMS is a station-level univariate margin,
not the peak/volume pair the copula stage needs; that requires a separate rainfall→discharge step.

**2. No single national ξ.** −0.19 to +0.40 across five stations rules out treating ξ as a fixed
national constant — the margin has to stay station-specific, or be pooled deliberately (regional
frequency analysis), consistent with the project's standing "report the spread, don't collapse to
one number" convention (Next Action 7).

**3. Same instability shape as Day 2's λ_U finding, one dimension down.** A parametric bootstrap on
ξ gives a 95% CI of (−0.25, 0.28) at Kathmandu but (0.09, 5.0) at Surkhet — pulled by a single
outlier year (423.1 mm vs. next-highest 281.2 mm). Day 2 showed a 40-year record can't resolve
$\lambda_U$ (a bivariate tail parameter) precisely; this shows the same limitation one level down,
for a univariate shape parameter. L-moments/PWM are flagged as the next thing to try — more stable
than MLE at this record length per the standard EVT literature (Coles 2001) — not yet implemented.
