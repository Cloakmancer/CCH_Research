## Day 1 — Distribution shape and skewness

*2026-07-28*

### Concept

Distribution shape: symmetric (Normal) versus right-skewed, and what the ordering of mean,
median and mode reveals about tail behaviour.

For a symmetric unimodal distribution, mean = median = mode. For a right-skewed distribution
the ordering is

$$\text{mode} < \text{median} < \text{mean}$$

because the mean is not resistant to the long upper tail: a few extreme observations pull it
above the median, while the median — a rank statistic — barely moves. The gap
$\bar{x} - \tilde{x}$ is therefore a crude but honest diagnostic of upper-tail mass.

![Normal versus right-skewed (Gamma) distribution, with mean, median and mode marked](images/skew-comparison.png)

*Normal (symmetric, mean = median = mode) against a right-skewed Gamma. Generated by
`skew_chart.py` in the study folder.*

### What it actually says

The two distributions differ in *tail decay*, not merely in appearance. A Normal density
decays as $\exp(-z^2/2)$ — superexponentially — so mass is concentrated within one to two
standard deviations of the mean and the probability of a large deviation collapses very
quickly. A right-skewed distribution of the Gamma type decays exponentially at worst, and the
heavy-tailed families used in hydrology (GEV with $\xi > 0$, log-Pearson III, log-normal)
decay slower still, polynomially. The difference in the far quantiles is not marginal; it is
orders of magnitude in exceedance probability.

### Bridge to CCH

River discharge is not normally distributed. Annual maxima in particular are right-skewed:
median discharge sits below the mean, and the mean is dragged upward by a small number of
large events. This has three consequences for the CCH workflow.

1. **A Normal assumption underestimates threshold exceedance.** If a cascading hazard is
   triggered when discharge crosses some threshold $q^*$ well into the upper tail, then
   $P(Q > q^*)$ computed under a fitted Normal is biased low — and the bias grows the further
   into the tail the threshold sits. Since the whole point of CCH analysis is behaviour at
   trigger thresholds, this is exactly where the error is largest and least tolerable.

2. **Central-tendency summaries are the wrong summary.** Reporting mean discharge describes a
   value that is neither typical (the median is lower) nor relevant to the hazard (the tail is
   what matters). For skewed hydrologic data, the median describes the ordinary year and the
   upper quantiles describe the hazard; the mean describes neither well.

3. **It motivates the copula machinery rather than merely decorating it.** The reason the
   project fits margins parametrically (GEV / LP3 / log-normal) and then works on
   pseudo-observations $u_i = R(x_i)/(n+1)$ is precisely that raw skewed margins cannot be
   assumed away. The rank transform removes the marginal shape problem from the dependence
   problem — but only the dependence problem. Marginal tail behaviour still has to be got
   right separately, and this is why margin misspecification was flagged as the reason for
   preferring semiparametric (pseudo-ML) fitting over IFM
   ([Research Log, 2026-07-26](research-log.md)).

The univariate lesson also previews the bivariate one: just as a Normal margin understates
single-variable extremes, a Gaussian *copula* — zero upper-tail dependence — understates the
probability of two drivers being extreme together. Same failure, one dimension up. That is the
argument for Gumbel as the lead family
([Knowledge Base](knowledge-base.md), [Revision Part 1](revision-copula-sph-lstm.md)).

### Feynman check — the whole thing without the notation

*If you cannot explain it plainly you have not understood it. Below is the plain version; the
sections above are the same argument with the machinery shown.*

Rivers do not do "average". Most years the flood is unremarkable; every so often one arrives that
is several times bigger than anything in living memory. Plot forty years of annual maxima and you
get a heap of ordinary years on the left with a few monsters straggling off to the right. That
straggle is the whole subject.

It has an immediate consequence for arithmetic. Those few monsters drag the **average** up, but they
barely move the **middle value** — one enormous flood shifts the middle by one place, not by a
thousand cubic metres. So in a skewed record the average sits above the middle, and quoting the
average describes a year that is wetter than most. It is not a typical year and it is not a
dangerous year; it describes nothing real.

Now the part that actually matters. A bell curve's edges die off *ferociously* fast — three
standard deviations out it already says "once in 740 years", four says "once in 31,600", five says
"forget it". Real rivers do not fall away anything like that quickly. So if you fit a bell curve to
flood data and ask how often some dangerous level gets crossed, the answer comes back far too
optimistic — and the further out you ask, the worse the answer gets. On the numbers in the
interactive page: at 2,200 m³/s the bell curve is wrong by a factor of 18; at 3,000 m³/s it is wrong
by a factor of 8,000.

That is fatal here specifically, because a compounding cascading hazard *is* a chain of thresholds.
The reservoir overtops, then the slope saturates, then it fails, then the debris dams the channel.
Every link is a "does it cross the line?" question, so the entire analysis lives in the part of the
distribution where the bell curve is most wrong. The error is not spread thinly across the whole
range — it is concentrated precisely on the number being computed. Design to it and you have built
for one event per century in a river that delivers three.

The copula work then does something clever about this. It throws away the actual discharge numbers
and keeps only each year's **rank** — smallest, second smallest, and so on, divided by n+1. That
erases the skew completely and leaves behind only the question of whether high peaks travel with
high volumes, which is the thing a copula is for. But it only erases half the problem: the moment
you convert back to real units to state a design discharge, the marginal distribution is needed
again, and picking it wrong ruins the answer no matter how good the copula is. Hence fitting the
copula on empirical ranks — so a bad guess about the margin cannot leak into the dependence
estimate.

And the last step is the one worth carrying forward. The mistake repeats itself one dimension up.
Just as a bell curve says big floods are rarer than they are, a **Gaussian copula** says two drivers
being extreme *together* is rarer than it is — it has the property that the further into the
extremes you look, the more independent it insists the two variables become. Peak and volume come
out of the same storm; they should bind together at the extremes, not drift apart. Gumbel does that,
with a fixed limiting value of 2 − √2 ≈ 0.59 no matter how far out you push, while the Gaussian's
equivalent number slides steadily toward zero. Same failure, one dimension up — which is why an
elementary lesson about skewness is not a detour from this project but a rehearsal of its central
argument.

### Open thread

* Skewness is descriptive, not a model. Quantifying "how heavy" requires the tail index
  $\xi$ of a GEV fit, not the skewness coefficient — worth doing on the Nepal rainfall series
  in `04_Resources/` once the study track reaches distribution fitting.
* Mean-versus-median gap is confounded with sample size for heavy tails: the sample mean of a
  heavy-tailed variable converges slowly and is itself unstable across records. Check how the
  gap behaves on the real series before treating it as a stable diagnostic.
