## 1.1 The problem copulas solve

Univariate flood frequency analysis answers: *how often is the peak discharge ≥ Q?* Real hazard is
rarely univariate. A levee fails on peak **and** sustained volume; a GLOF cascade depends on
lake-level **and** moraine saturation **and** seismic trigger. You need a joint distribution.

The obstacle: marginals in hydrology are heterogeneous and heavy-tailed (peaks ~ GEV, volumes ~
Gamma or lognormal, durations ~ exponential-ish). Classical multivariate families force a common
marginal type — bivariate normal forces both margins normal, bivariate gamma forces both gamma.
That is a modelling straitjacket unrelated to the physics.

**The copula move:** separate the *marginal behaviour* of each variable from the *dependence
structure* linking them. Model each margin however the data demands; model the dependence with a
separate function. Then glue.

## 1.2 Sklar's theorem — the load-bearing result

For a joint CDF *H* with margins *F* and *G*, there exists a copula *C* : [0,1]² → [0,1] such that

$$H(x,y) = C(F(x), G(y))$$

and if *F*, *G* are continuous, *C* is **unique**. Conversely, given any margins and any copula,
that formula produces a valid joint CDF.

Read it twice. Two claims:

- **Decomposition (⇒):** every continuous joint distribution *already contains* a copula. The
  copula is not an approximation you impose; it is extracted.
- **Construction (⇐):** you can mix and match freely. GEV peak + Gamma volume + Gumbel copula is a
  legitimate joint distribution.

**Why the unit square.** The probability integral transform: if *X* ~ *F* continuous, then
*U* = *F*(*X*) ~ Uniform(0,1). So *C* is just the joint CDF of the two rank-transformed variables.
A copula is a joint distribution whose margins are both standard uniform — by construction it
carries *only* dependence information, having had all marginal information transformed away.

The density follows by differentiation:

$$h(x,y) = c(F(x),G(y))\cdot f(x) \cdot g(y)$$

The likelihood factorises into a marginal part and a dependence part — which is exactly what makes
two-step (IFM) estimation legitimate. See §1.7.

**Copula density is not a probability.** $c(u,v)$ can exceed 1. It is a density on the unit square,
and it is the ratio of joint density to the product of marginals — i.e. a *dependence multiplier*.
$c(u,v) = 1$ everywhere $\Leftrightarrow$ independence.

**Invariance.** *C* is invariant to strictly increasing transformations of the margins. Log-transform
your discharge; the copula is unchanged. This is a strong property and the reason copulas are the
natural language for dependence.

**Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds.** $\max(u+v-1,0) \le C(u,v) \le \min(u,v)$. Lower bound *W* = perfect negative
dependence (countermonotonicity), upper bound *M* = perfect positive dependence (comonotonicity),
independence Π = uv sits between. Any copula lives in this envelope. In 3+ dimensions the lower
bound is no longer a copula — an early sign that high dimensions are harder.

## 1.3 Dependence measures — and why Pearson is out

**Pearson's r** measures *linear* association and depends on the margins. Under monotone
transformation it changes. It can also be constrained: for two lognormals with different σ, the
attainable range of *r* excludes ±1 entirely. It is a property of the joint distribution, not of the
dependence structure. Wrong tool.

**Kendall's τ** — concordance-based, rank-based, margin-free, a functional of the copula alone:

$$\tau = 4\iint C(u,v)\, dC(u,v) - 1$$

Sample version: $\hat\tau = (\#\text{concordant} - \#\text{discordant}) / \binom{n}{2}$. A pair $(i,j)$ is concordant if
$(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j) > 0$.

**Spearman's ρ_S** — Pearson's r applied to ranks; also a copula functional:
$\rho_S = 12\iint uv\, dC(u,v) - 3$. Weights the middle of the distribution more; τ is generally preferred
in hydrology for its cleaner analytic links to θ and its robustness.

Both are 0 under independence, ±1 under comonotonicity/countermonotonicity, and invariant to
monotone margin transforms.

> **Q1 answer.** Yes — Y = X² with X symmetric about 0 gives r = 0 and perfect functional
> dependence. Correlation ≠ dependence. Copulas exist precisely because the two are distinct.

## 1.4 Tail dependence — the concept that matters for CCH

Everything above concerns *average* dependence. Hazard cascades are about *extremes co-occurring*.

Upper tail dependence coefficient:

$$\lambda_U = \lim_{u\to 1^-} P(V>u \mid U>u) = \lim_{u\to 1^-}\frac{1-2u+C(u,u)}{1-u}$$

Plain reading: *given that one variable is at its q-th percentile with q → 1, what is the limiting
probability that the other is too?* λ_U ∈ [0,1]. λ_U = 0 is **asymptotic independence**: extremes
eventually decouple. λ_U > 0 is **asymptotic dependence**: extremes co-occur with non-vanishing
probability no matter how far into the tail you go.

Lower tail dependence λ_L is the mirror image (relevant for compound drought, low-flow).

**Why this is the crux of your project.** Compound hazard risk is dominated by λ_U. Two copulas can
agree on τ to three decimals and differ by an order of magnitude in the joint exceedance probability
of the design event. Fit statistics computed over the whole sample are dominated by the ~95% of data
that is not extreme, so they are close to uninformative about λ_U. This is the standard critique
(Serinaldi) of naive copula selection in hydrology: **the tail is where the risk is and where the
data are not.**

> **Q4 answer.** The Gaussian copula has λ_U = 0 for every ρ < 1. It is asymptotically independent.
> Fit it to peak–volume data and you will systematically *underestimate* the probability of extreme
> peak co-occurring with extreme volume — the exact scenario a dam-safety study exists to quantify.
> Passing a global GoF test does not rescue it. This is the same structural error as the Gaussian
> copula in CDO pricing before 2008.

## 1.5 Families — what to know about each

### Elliptical

| Family | λ_L, λ_U | τ ↔ parameter | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | 0, 0 | $\tau = (2/\pi)\arcsin\rho$ | Symmetric, no tail dep. Default that should rarely survive scrutiny. |
| Student-t | equal, > 0 | same τ relation | Symmetric tail dependence, controlled by ν. λ → 0 as ν → ∞. Good default when tails matter but asymmetry doesn't. |

Student-t $\lambda_U = 2\, t_{\nu+1}\!\left(-\sqrt{(\nu+1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\right)$. Note λ_U > 0 even when ρ = 0 — uncorrelated but
tail-dependent. Worth internalising.

### Archimedean

Built from a generator $\varphi: [0,1] \to [0,\infty]$, convex, decreasing, $\varphi(1) = 0$:

$$C(u,v) = \varphi^{-1}\big(\varphi(u)+\varphi(v)\big)$$

One parameter, closed form, easy simulation, and — critically for hydrology — **asymmetric tail
behaviour**.

| Family | Generator φ(t) | θ range | τ | λ_L | λ_U |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gumbel–Hougaard** | $(-\ln t)^\theta$ | $[1,\infty)$ | $1-1/\theta$ | $0$ | $2-2^{1/\theta}$ |
| **Clayton** | $(t^{-\theta}-1)/\theta$ | $(0,\infty)$ | $\theta/(\theta+2)$ | $2^{-1/\theta}$ | $0$ |
| **Frank** | $-\ln\!\big[(e^{-\theta t}-1)/(e^{-\theta}-1)\big]$ | $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ | Debye-function form | $0$ | $0$ |
| **Joe** | $-\ln\!\big[1-(1-t)^\theta\big]$ | $[1,\infty)$ | no closed form | $0$ | $2-2^{1/\theta}$ |

**Gumbel is the workhorse for flood peak–volume.** Upper tail dependence, no lower tail dependence
— matches the physical intuition that big peaks come with big volumes, while the low-flow end
decouples. It is also the only Archimedean family that is an *extreme-value copula*, i.e. stable
under the max operation: C(u^t, v^t) = C(u,v)^t. If your marginals are block maxima (annual maxima),
extreme-value-copula theory says the dependence structure of the maxima should itself be EV.
That is a *theoretical* argument for Gumbel, not merely an empirical one — use it.

**Clayton** is Gumbel's mirror: lower tail dependence. Use for compound low-flow/drought, not for
flood peaks. **Frank** has neither tail; it is the Archimedean analogue of "symmetric, no tail" and
is the only common one admitting negative dependence.

> **Q6 answer.** θ = 3 in Gumbel ⇒ τ = 1 − 1/3 = 0.667 (strong positive concordance) and
> λ_U = 2 − 2^{1/3} = 0.74. Three-quarters of the time that one variable is extreme, so is the other,
> in the limit. θ = 1 recovers independence; θ → ∞ approaches comonotonicity.

### Others worth knowing exist
Survival/rotated copulas (rotate Clayton 180° to get upper tail dependence with a different shape),
BB1/BB7 two-parameter families (independent control of λ_L and λ_U), and empirical copulas
(nonparametric, useful as a GoF reference).

## 1.6 Pseudo-observations

You never fit a copula to raw data. You fit it to ranks:

$$u_i = \frac{R(x_i)}{n+1}, \qquad v_i = \frac{R(y_i)}{n+1}$$

The (n+1) denominator — not n — keeps values strictly inside (0,1), avoiding infinite density
evaluations at the boundary. These are the **pseudo-observations**. Everything downstream
(τ estimation, MLE, GoF) operates on them.

Two philosophies:
- **Semiparametric / pseudo-ML (recommended default):** empirical ranks for margins, parametric
  copula. Robust — margin misspecification cannot contaminate the copula estimate.
- **Fully parametric / IFM:** fit parametric margins first, transform with the fitted CDFs, then fit
  the copula. More efficient *if* margins are right, biased if not. In flood work, margin choice
  (GEV vs LP3 vs GLO) is itself contested, so the semiparametric route is safer.

## 1.7 Estimation

**Method of moments via τ.** Invert the τ–θ relation. Gumbel: $\hat\theta = 1/(1-\hat\tau)$. Clayton:
$\hat\theta = 2\hat\tau/(1-\hat\tau)$. Fast, robust, no optimiser. Excellent starting value; adequate final answer for
one-parameter families in small samples. Note it is a *rank-based* estimator, hence margin-free.

**Pseudo-maximum likelihood.** Maximise $\sum \log c(u_i, v_i; \theta)$ over θ using pseudo-observations.
Asymptotically more efficient. Standard errors need care — the plug-in of empirical margins inflates
variance beyond the naive Hessian estimate, so use a bootstrap.

**Sanity check.** MOM and PML estimates of θ should be close. A large gap usually indicates
family misspecification, not an optimiser problem.

## 1.8 Goodness of fit and selection

- **AIC / BIC** on the copula log-likelihood. Comparative only, and — repeat — dominated by the body
  of the distribution.
- **Cramér–von Mises statistic** $S_n = \int (C_n - C_{\hat\theta})^2\, dC_n$, with a **parametric bootstrap** p-value.
  You cannot use the asymptotic null distribution because θ is estimated. Genest, Rémillard &
  Beaudoin (2009) is the reference.
- **K-plot and Chi-plot** — graphical, sensitive to different departures.
- **Tail-focused diagnostics** — plot the empirical $\lambda_U(u) = [1-2u+C_n(u,u)]/(1-u)$ against u for
  u ∈ [0.9, 0.99] and overlay the candidate families. This is the diagnostic that discriminates the
  models you actually care about. Do it.
- **Report sensitivity, don't just select.** Best practice in compound-hazard work is to carry 2–3
  plausible families through to the design-value stage and report the spread. Selecting one family
  and reporting a single design event conceals the dominant source of uncertainty.

## 1.9 Multivariate return periods — get this right

Univariate: $T = \mu / (1-F(x))$, with μ the mean inter-arrival time (1 year for annual maxima).
Bivariate, with $u = F(x)$, $v = G(y)$:

**OR ("either exceeds")** — $P(X>x \text{ or } Y>y) = 1 - C(u,v)$

$$T_{OR} = \frac{\mu}{1-C(u,v)}$$

**AND ("both exceed")** — $P(X>x \text{ and } Y>y) = 1-u-v+C(u,v)$

$$T_{AND} = \frac{\mu}{1-u-v+C(u,v)}$$

Since the AND event is a subset of the OR event, P_AND ≤ P_OR, hence **T_AND ≥ T_OR** always.

> **Q5 answer.** AND is rarer, so its return period is longer. Which you use is a *failure-mechanism*
> question, not a statistical one:
> - A levee overtopped by peak stage alone, or breached by prolonged saturation alone → **OR**.
> - A structure that only fails when high peak coincides with high volume → **AND**.
> Using AND where the physics is OR gives a dangerously long return period and an undersized design.

**Both are non-unique.** For a given T there is an entire *isoline* (level curve) of (x, y) pairs,
not one design event. Practical resolutions:

1. **Most-likely design event** — the point on the isoline maximising the joint density h(x,y).
2. **Kendall return period** — use the Kendall distribution function $K_C(t) = P(C(U,V) \le t)$ to define
   a univariate probability for the multivariate event; $T_K = \mu/(1-K_C(t))$. For Archimedean
   copulas $K_C(t) = t - \varphi(t)/\varphi'(t)$, which is why Archimedeans are convenient here.
3. **Structural / survival Kendall** — Salvadori's approach for the "hazard scenario" framing.
4. **Failure probability with a structure function** — if you have a physical response model, define
   the critical region directly and integrate the copula over it. Most defensible when available.

Salvadori & De Michele (2004, 2010) and Gräler et al. (2013) are the citations here.

## 1.10 Beyond bivariate: vines

Archimedean copulas extend to d dimensions only by imposing exchangeability — every pair shares one
θ. For CCH (rainfall, discharge, sediment, lake level, seismicity) that is untenable.

**Vine (pair-copula) construction:** factorise the d-dimensional density into $d(d-1)/2$ bivariate
copulas, some unconditional, some conditional. Each pair gets its own family and parameter, so you
can put Gumbel on the rainfall–discharge edge and Frank on another. Two canonical structures:

- **C-vine** — a hub node drives each tree. Use when one variable governs (e.g. rainfall).
- **D-vine** — path structure. Use for temporally/spatially ordered variables.
- **R-vine** — general; structure selected by maximum-spanning-tree on |τ| (Dißmann's algorithm).

`pyvinecopulib` implements all of this: structure selection, family selection per edge, and
sequential + joint MLE. Costs: parameter proliferation, structure-selection uncertainty, and the
**simplifying assumption** (conditional copulas do not depend on the conditioning value) — which is
almost always made and almost never tested. Know that this is the standing critique of vines.

## 1.11 Non-stationarity — the frontier you should acknowledge

Everything above assumes iid. Under climate change neither margins nor dependence are stable.
Options: time-varying θ(t) via covariates, conditional copulas, or a Bayesian dynamic copula.
Serinaldi & Kilsby's warning applies: adding non-stationarity adds parameters and often *increases*
predictive uncertainty rather than reducing it. Mention this in a viva; don't overclaim it.

## 2.1 The core idea

Mesh methods track field values at fixed points in space (Eulerian) and struggle when the domain
itself deforms violently — breaking waves, dam breaks, debris flow, GLOF surge overtopping a moraine.
Free surfaces must be reconstructed (VOF, level set), and large deformation wrecks a Lagrangian mesh.

SPH discretises the *fluid* rather than the *space*. The continuum becomes a set of particles that
carry mass, density, velocity, pressure and move with the flow. There is no mesh, hence no mesh
tangling, and the free surface is simply where the particles stop. This is why SPH is the natural
choice for GLOF surge, dam-break, and impulse-wave problems.

The cost, stated plainly: SPH is far more expensive per unit accuracy than a good mesh method for
smooth flows, its convergence is harder to establish, and pressure fields are noisy. It earns its
place only when the geometry/free surface makes meshing impractical.

## 2.2 Kernel interpolation — the whole method in two lines

Start from the exact identity using the Dirac delta:

$$A(\mathbf{r}) = \int A(\mathbf{r}')\,\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}')\, d\mathbf{r}'$$

Replace δ with a smooth, compactly supported kernel $W(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}', h)$:

$$A(\mathbf{r}) \approx \int A(\mathbf{r}')\, W(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}', h)\, d\mathbf{r}'$$
(kernel approximation, $O(h^2)$ if $W$ is even)

Discretise the integral as a sum over neighbouring particles, with volume element
$d\mathbf{r}' \to m_j/\rho_j$:

$$A_i \approx \sum_j \frac{m_j}{\rho_j} A_j W_{ij}$$
(particle approximation)

That is SPH. Every operator follows from it.

**Kernel requirements:** normalisation ∫W = 1; compact support (radius κh, typically 2h);
positivity; monotone decrease; and δ-convergence as h → 0. Common choices: cubic spline
(cheap, support 2h), quintic spline and Wendland C² (better stability, fewer pairing instabilities).
DualSPHysics defaults to Wendland.

**Smoothing length h** sets the resolution: it is the physical radius over which properties are
averaged, analogous to mesh spacing. Halving h sharpens resolution but (a) shrinks the neighbour
radius, so to keep the neighbour count you must also refine particle spacing dp — in 3D that is
8× the particles — and (b) reduces the CFL time step. Cost scales roughly h⁻⁴ in 3D. Typical
h/dp ≈ 1.2–1.5, giving ~50 (2D) or ~250 (3D) neighbours.

**The gradient trick.** Differentiate the interpolant, and the derivative lands entirely on the
kernel, which is analytic:

$$\nabla A_i \approx \sum_j \frac{m_j}{\rho_j} A_j \nabla W_{ij}$$

No finite differences, no stencil, no mesh. This is the single most elegant point in SPH — and
the point to state first if asked to explain the method.

In practice the naive gradient is not used; symmetrised forms are, because they conserve momentum
exactly (pairwise forces equal and opposite by antisymmetry of ∇W_ij):

$$\nabla A_i \approx \rho_i \sum_j m_j\left[\frac{A_i}{\rho_i^2}+\frac{A_j}{\rho_j^2}\right]\nabla W_{ij}$$

## 2.3 The discretised governing equations

**Continuity** (density by summation, or by rate of change):

$$\frac{d\rho_i}{dt} = \sum_j m_j (\mathbf{v}_i-\mathbf{v}_j)\cdot \nabla W_{ij}$$

**Momentum:**

$$\frac{d\mathbf{v}_i}{dt} = -\sum_j m_j\left[\frac{p_i}{\rho_i^2}+\frac{p_j}{\rho_j^2}+\Pi_{ij}\right]\nabla W_{ij} + \mathbf{g}$$

Π_ij is the artificial viscosity term (Monaghan): it prevents particle interpenetration and damps
post-shock oscillation. It is numerical, not physical — its coefficient α is a tuning parameter,
and its magnitude should be reported and justified. Alternatives: laminar viscosity + SPS
(sub-particle scale) turbulence, which DualSPHysics supports and which is preferable for
engineering work where the effective viscosity matters.

**Position:** $\dfrac{d\mathbf{r}_i}{dt} = \mathbf{v}_i$, optionally with XSPH correction (velocity smoothed by neighbours) to
keep particle distribution orderly.

## 2.4 Closing the system: WCSPH vs ISPH

Water is nearly incompressible, so there is no thermodynamic equation of state to close on. Two routes:

**WCSPH (weakly compressible).** Impose an artificial stiff equation of state — Tait/Cole:

$$p = \frac{c_0^2 \rho_0}{\gamma}\left[\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_0}\right)^\gamma - 1\right], \qquad \gamma = 7$$

Choose the numerical speed of sound $c_0 \ge 10 \cdot v_{max}$ so that density fluctuations stay under ~1%
(Mach ≤ 0.1). Pressure becomes an explicit local function of density — no global solve, trivially
parallel, GPU-friendly. **This is what DualSPHysics does.** Downside: pressure field is noisy
because it is a stiff function of a noisy density, and the acoustic CFL condition
$\Delta t \lesssim 0.25\, h/c_0$ makes the time step small.

**ISPH (incompressible).** Enforce ∇·v = 0 exactly by solving a pressure Poisson equation each step
(projection method). Smooth pressure, larger time steps — but a global linear solve every step,
which parallelises badly. Preferred when pressure accuracy (e.g. impact loads on a structure) is the
deliverable.

> **Q3 answer.** Weak compressibility trades a small, controlled physical error (≈1% density
> variation) for the removal of a global elliptic solve. It converts an implicit problem into an
> explicit one, which is what makes GPU SPH viable.

## 2.5 Boundary conditions — the hard part

A particle near a wall has an incomplete neighbour set (kernel support truncated), so its density
sum is deficient and it drifts toward the boundary. No mesh means no natural place to impose a BC.

- **Dynamic boundary particles (DBC)** — DualSPHysics default. Wall particles obey the same
  continuity and state equations as fluid but are fixed (or move by prescribed motion). Cheap,
  robust, but produces an unphysical gap layer and pressure noise near walls. *modified DBC (mDBC)*
  in recent DualSPHysics substantially improves this — use it.
- **Repulsive/Lennard-Jones boundaries** — explicit force law; simple, but the force is arbitrary.
- **Ghost/mirror particles** — reflect fluid across the wall to complete the kernel support.
  Accurate; awkward for complex 3D geometry.
- **Inlet/outlet** — buffer zones with prescribed velocity or pressure. Needed for river reach
  simulation; historically a weak point of SPH.

> **Q4 answer.** In a mesh code a wall is a face of the grid and the BC is imposed on that face. In
> SPH the wall has no discrete representation, and the kernel does not know it exists — it must be
> reconstructed by artificial particles or forces, all of which are approximations.

## 2.6 Stability, time stepping, accuracy

- **Time integration:** explicit — Verlet or symplectic. Δt from the minimum of the CFL condition
  ($h/c_0$), the viscous condition ($h^2/\nu$), and the force condition ($\sqrt{h/|f|}$).
- **Tensile instability** — under tension particles clump into unphysical pairs. Mitigations:
  artificial stress, Wendland kernels, or corrected formulations.
- **Particle disorder** — the particle approximation only recovers O(h²) for a regular distribution;
  disorder degrades it toward O(1) consistency loss. Remedies: XSPH, particle shifting, or corrected
  kernel gradients (CSPM/MLS).
- **Density drift** — cured by δ-SPH (a diffusive term in the continuity equation) or periodic
  density reinitialisation (Shepard/MLS filter). δ-SPH is the modern standard and markedly cleans up
  the pressure field.
- **Convergence** — with disorder, δ-SPH, artificial viscosity and boundary error all interacting,
  formal convergence order is rarely observed. Publishable SPH work therefore *requires* a resolution
  study (typically 3 dp values) and validation against experiment.

> **Q5 answer (blow-up).** (i) Δt violating CFL, often because c₀ was raised without recomputing Δt;
> (ii) boundary penetration — particles tunnelling through a wall due to too-low c₀ or a poor BC;
> (iii) tensile instability / particle clumping; also insufficient artificial viscosity, or an
> initial particle configuration not in hydrostatic equilibrium.

## 2.7 DualSPHysics in practice

Workflow: `XML case definition` → **GenCase** (generates initial particle configuration from
geometry + fill definitions) → **DualSPHysics** (CPU/GPU solver) → post-processors
(**PartVTK** for particle fields, **MeasureTool** for probes/gauges, **IsoSurface** for free surface)
→ ParaView.

Decisions to be able to justify in a viva:
- `dp` (initial particle spacing) — the single dominant cost/accuracy knob; N ∝ dp⁻³.
- `coefh` (h/dp ratio), kernel choice (Wendland).
- `coefsound` (c₀ relative to max expected velocity) — check the 10× rule against your actual v_max.
- viscosity treatment: artificial (α) vs laminar+SPS, and the value chosen.
- δ-SPH coefficient, boundary method (prefer mDBC).
- Validation case before the real case. For GLOF/impulse-wave work the standard benchmarks are
  the dam-break-over-dry-bed experiments and the Heller–Hager landslide-generated-impulse-wave data.

Realistic scoping note: 3D SPH at engineering resolution is ~10⁶–10⁸ particles. GPU is not optional.

## 3.1 Why sequences need a different architecture

Discharge today depends on rainfall today, yesterday, last week, and — via soil moisture, snowpack,
groundwater — last season. A feed-forward net on a fixed window of the last *k* days has: a fixed
memory horizon chosen a priori, parameters that scale with *k*, and no weight sharing across time
(the same rainfall event is learned separately at every lag position). Catchment memory is a *state*,
not a window.

An RNN maintains a hidden state carried forward:

$$h_t = \tanh(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b)$$

$$\hat{y}_t = W_y h_t + b_y$$

Weights are shared across time steps; the sequence can be any length; the state is an internal
summary of everything seen so far. **Structural analogy worth using in writing:** h_t is a learned
analogue of the storage states in a conceptual rainfall–runoff model (HBV, Sacramento), except the
storages and their transfer functions are learned rather than prescribed.

Training is **backpropagation through time** (BPTT): unroll the network over the sequence and apply
standard backprop; gradients for shared weights are summed over time steps.

## 3.2 The vanishing/exploding gradient problem

The gradient of a loss at time *T* with respect to a state at time *t* involves the product

$$\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} = \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = \prod \text{diag}(\tanh') \cdot W_h^\top$$

A product of $(T-t)$ matrices. Governed by the largest singular value of $W_h$:

- < 1 → the product decays geometrically → **vanishing gradient**. Long-range dependencies contribute
  nothing to the update. The network can still be trained; it simply *cannot learn* long-range
  structure.
- \> 1 → geometric growth → **exploding gradient**, NaNs. Cheaply patched by gradient clipping.

Compounding this, tanh′ ≤ 1 always, so the diagonal factor only ever shrinks the product.

> **Q2 answer.** It is a product, not a sum. Anything repeatedly multiplied either dies or diverges;
> the stable middle is a knife-edge. Vanishing is the serious one because it fails silently — you get
> a model that trains fine and has no long memory. For a catchment with seasonal storage, that is
> exactly the failure that matters.

## 3.3 LSTM: the additive memory path

Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997). The fix: add a second state, the **cell state c_t**, whose default
dynamics are *additive* rather than multiplicative:

$$c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t$$

If the forget gate $f_t \approx 1$, $c_t \approx c_{t-1} + \text{(new information)}$. Gradient flows back along this path
by repeated multiplication by $f_t \approx 1$ rather than by $W_h$ — the **constant error carousel**. This is
the whole idea; the gates are machinery around it.

Full equations ($\odot$ = elementwise, $\sigma$ = logistic, all gates take $[h_{t-1}, x_t]$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma\big(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f\big) &&\text{(forget gate)}\\
i_t &= \sigma\big(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i\big) &&\text{(input gate)}\\
\tilde{c}_t &= \tanh\big(W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c\big) &&\text{(candidate cell state)}\\
c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t &&\text{(cell update)}\\
o_t &= \sigma\big(W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o\big) &&\text{(output gate)}\\
h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) &&\text{(hidden state)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Gates are σ-valued in (0,1): differentiable soft switches, one value per cell dimension. They are
*learned, input-dependent* — the same LSTM can hold information during a dry spell and flush it at
the onset of a storm, because the gates are functions of the current input.

**Reading the three gates hydrologically** (this framing is well supported in the literature —
Kratzert et al. showed LSTM cell states correlate with observed snow water equivalent and soil
moisture without ever being trained on them):

- **forget** — how much stored catchment state to carry forward (recession, depletion).
- **input** — how much of the current forcing to write into storage (infiltration, accumulation).
- **output** — how much of the internal store to expose as discharge right now (release).

> **Q3 answer.** c_t is long-term memory — a protected linear conveyor, only modified by gates.
> h_t is the working/exposed state — a filtered view of c_t used for prediction and fed to the next
> step's gates. Separating them lets the network *hold* information it is not currently *using*.
>
> **Q4 answer.** The forget gate, at ≈1 for the relevant cell dimensions across the whole
> accumulation season.

**Parameter count:** $4 \cdot (n_h \cdot (n_h + n_x) + n_h)$. The factor 4 is the three gates plus the
candidate — a useful check when debugging model size.

**Standard trick:** initialise b_f to +1 (or +2). Early in training this puts the forget gate near
open, so gradients propagate and the model can discover long dependencies before it decides to
forget.

## 3.4 GRU and the comparison

GRU merges cell and hidden state and uses two gates (update z_t, reset r_t):

$$h_t = (1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t$$

Fewer parameters (3 gate-blocks vs 4), faster, often comparable. Empirically in hydrology LSTM has
a small but consistent edge on long-memory catchments (snow, deep groundwater). Defensible position:
default to LSTM, report GRU as an ablation.

## 3.5 Architectures for hydrological forecasting

- **Sequence-to-one.** Input the last *T* days of forcings (precip, temperature, PET, radiation),
  output discharge at day *T*. The standard rainfall–runoff setup. Lookback *T* = 270–365 days is the
  usual choice — it must exceed the catchment's memory (snow requires a full year).
- **Sequence-to-sequence / encoder–decoder.** Encoder consumes observed history; decoder emits a
  multi-day forecast, optionally conditioned on forecast forcings (NWP). This is the flood-forecasting
  architecture.
- **Multi-timescale / MTS-LSTM** — mixed hourly/daily inputs.
- **Regional (multi-catchment) LSTM — the important result.** Train one LSTM across hundreds of
  catchments, passing static catchment attributes (area, slope, soil, land cover, aridity) alongside
  the dynamic forcings. Kratzert et al. (2019) showed this *outperforms* per-catchment calibrated
  conceptual models **and** per-catchment LSTMs, and generalises to ungauged basins — a serious result
  for Nepal, where gauge density is the binding constraint. Cite this if you propose LSTM for
  ungauged Himalayan catchments.
- **Hybrid / physics-informed** — LSTM as a residual on a conceptual model, or with mass-balance
  constraints imposed on the cell state (mass-conserving LSTM). This is the current frontier and the
  natural answer to "isn't it a black box?"

## 3.6 Training practicalities

- **Loss.** MSE overweights high flows. Common alternatives: NSE-based loss (Kratzert's basin-averaged
  NSE loss, which stops large-discharge basins from dominating a regional model), or MSE on
  log-transformed or Box–Cox-transformed discharge if low flows matter.
- **Normalisation.** Standardise inputs; log-transform discharge (it is right-skewed and non-negative).
  Compute statistics **on the training split only**.
- **Splitting.** Strictly chronological — train early, validate middle, test late. Random shuffling of
  time steps leaks future into past and is the single most common fatal error in hydrological ML
  papers. For regional models, also hold out *catchments* to test spatial generalisation.
- **Warm-up.** Discard predictions from the first ~lookback steps of each sequence; the state is
  uninitialised.
- **Regularisation.** Dropout on the *non-recurrent* connections (or variational dropout with a shared
  mask across time — do not apply an independent dropout mask per time step to the recurrent path, it
  destroys memory). Early stopping on validation NSE.
- **Optimiser.** Adam, lr 1e-3 with decay; gradient clipping at norm ~1.
- **Ensembles.** Train 8–10 seeds and average. Reliably worth ~0.02–0.03 NSE and gives a crude
  uncertainty band, though a proper predictive distribution needs MC-dropout, quantile regression,
  or a mixture-density head.

## 3.7 Evaluation — and the trap

Metrics: **NSE** $= 1 - \dfrac{\sum(Q_{obs}-Q_{sim})^2}{\sum(Q_{obs}-\bar{Q}_{obs})^2}$; NSE = 0 means "no better than the
observed mean". **KGE** decomposes into correlation, bias and variability ratio and is generally
preferred. Add **peak flow bias** and **timing error** explicitly — a flood-forecasting model is
judged on peaks, and NSE can hide a systematic peak underestimate.

> **Q5 answer.** (i) NSE is inflated by strong seasonality — a model predicting the seasonal climatology
> scores respectably; always report NSE against a seasonal-climatology baseline, not just the mean.
> (ii) It is an in-catchment, in-period score; nothing about it demonstrates transferability.
> (iii) Extremes are rare, so a good NSE is compatible with poor performance on the events of interest
> — report peak-flow metrics separately.
> (iv) If any preprocessing (normalisation, imputation, feature selection) used the full record, the
> score is contaminated.

## 3.8 Honest limitations

Extrapolation beyond the training range is not reliable — an LSTM trained on a record whose maximum
is a 20-year flood has no basis for a 200-year event. Non-stationarity (land-use change, glacier
retreat) violates the iid assumption that training relies on. Data hunger is real, which is precisely
why regional pooling matters in data-sparse Nepal. And interpretability, while better than
commonly claimed (cell states are inspectable and do align with physical stores), is still weaker
than a conceptual model's.

# PART 4 — Where the three meet in CCH

The three are not parallel topics; they occupy different rungs of the same risk chain.

| | Copula | SPH | LSTM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | *How likely is this combination of drivers?* | *What does the flow physically do?* | *What will discharge be, given forcings?* |
| Type | Probabilistic / statistical | Deterministic / mechanistic | Data-driven / statistical |
| Inputs | Historical multivariate extremes | Geometry, initial conditions, material properties | Forcing time series + static attributes |
| Output | Joint exceedance probability, design event | Depth, velocity, impact pressure, inundation | Discharge hydrograph |
| Strength | Correct joint tail behaviour | Violent free-surface flow, no meshing | Cheap, accurate, learns catchment memory |
| Weakness | No physics; tail estimated from few points | Cost; boundary treatment; noisy pressure | No extrapolation; needs data |

A coherent CCH pipeline: **copula** generates joint design scenarios (peak–volume pairs, or
lake-level × trigger combinations) on a chosen return-period isoline → those scenarios become
**boundary/initial conditions for SPH** simulations of the resulting surge or breach → the resulting
inundation/impact metrics are combined with the scenario probabilities to produce a **risk** rather
than a hazard estimate. **LSTM** enters either upstream (generating discharge series where gauges are
absent, so the copula has data to fit) or as an emulator of the expensive SPH stage, enabling
Monte-Carlo over the full scenario space that direct simulation cannot afford.

That last framing — LSTM (or any surrogate) as an emulator letting you propagate copula-sampled
uncertainty through an expensive physical model — is the strongest single-sentence statement of how
the three combine, and worth having ready.
