### Why These Three Connect

- SPH generates high-fidelity synthetic training data for AI models (rare compound events have few observations)
- AI learns to predict cascade trigger conditions that SPH takes days to simulate
- CCH probabilistic frameworks define the joint hazard scenarios that SPH and AI must evaluate
- A unified framework: AI for fast first-pass prediction → SPH for detailed inundation physics → CCH for hazard scenario generation

### Civil Engineering Framing

Traditional design standards (ASCE 7, Eurocode 1, IS 1893) are fundamentally **univariate** — they specify a design flood, wind, or earthquake independently. The CCH literature is a direct critique of this: real hazards co-occur, interact, and cascade. The gap between what codes assume and what physics produces is itself a publishable research observation.

## 2. Compound Cascading Hazard (CCH) Typology

### Source: Zscheischler et al. (2020) — *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment*

The paper proposes four categories. Most real events span more than one type simultaneously. The typology is a **thinking tool**, not a rigid classification.

---

### Type 1: Preconditioned Events

**Definition:** A weather-driven or climate-driven precondition aggravates the impacts of a hazard. The hazard itself may be unremarkable — the antecedent state amplifies it.

**Mechanism:** Background state altered *before* the triggering hazard arrives.

**Civil engineering examples:**
- Rainfall on saturated soil → near-total runoff, drainage overwhelmed
- Rain-on-snow (ROS) → simultaneous melt + rainfall runoff (relevant to Nepal mid-hill catchments)
- Post-wildfire rainfall → debris flow on denuded slopes
- Drought-cracked soil → paradoxically high runoff when rain arrives

**Modeling approach:** Bivariate copula on (antecedent wetness index, event rainfall). API or soil moisture as conditioning variable. Cannot be captured by univariate flood frequency analysis (FFA).

---

### Type 2: Multivariate Events

**Definition:** Multiple concurrent drivers and/or hazards lead to an impact at about the same location. Hazards co-occur in space and time, usually driven by a common large-scale meteorological forcing.

**Civil engineering examples:**
- Storm surge + heavy rainfall → coastal river cannot drain (Nepal Terai, Bangladesh delta)
- Wave height + surge + astronomical tide → combined coastal water level exceeds freeboard
- High river discharge + high groundwater → scour + reduced abutment resistance at bridges
- Heat + drought → simultaneous demand spike + reduced hydroelectric supply

**Modeling approach:** Core domain of copula methods. Gumbel copula for upper tail dependence, vine copulas for >2 variables. AND vs OR return period choice is critical for structural design.

---

### Type 3: Temporally Compounding Events

**Definition:** A succession of hazards leads to an impact — recovery between events is incomplete. The second hazard finds the system in a weakened or depleted state.

**Key concept — recovery time:** If the inter-event interval is shorter than the system's recovery time, successive events cause disproportionate damage.

**Civil engineering examples:**
- Successive monsoon floods → embankment weakened by first event fails in second
- Earthquake → flood → bridge pier already weakened by seismic shaking
- Storm cluster → repeated wave loading fatigues coastal structure
- Flood → heatwave → damaged housing stock + heat exposure amplifies casualties

**Modeling approach:** Markov chain models, time-varying reliability functions, accumulated damage state tracking. Copulas can handle the event pair but the temporal dimension needs explicit damage accumulation logic.

---

### Type 4: Spatially Compounding Events

**Definition:** Hazards in multiple connected locations cause an aggregated impact. No single location alone explains the systemic consequence.

**Civil engineering examples:**
- Simultaneous bridge closures across Koshi + Gandaki + Karnali basins → road network collapse
- Multi-basin flooding in Nepal Terai → flood extents from separate catchments merge
- City-wide drainage failure under areally extensive rainfall → no dry sub-catchment to absorb overflow
- Simultaneous dam stress across a cascade system on the same river

**Modeling approach:** Vine copulas or spatial extreme value theory for multi-site dependence. Graph-theoretic network models for infrastructure failure propagation.

---

### CCH Typology → Modeling Tool Mapping

| Type | Primary statistical challenge | Recommended tool |
|---|---|---|
| Preconditioned | Conditioning on antecedent state | Conditional copulas, logistic regression on precondition |
| Multivariate | Joint exceedance of simultaneous variables | Bivariate / vine copulas, joint return periods |
| Temporally compounding | Dependence over time, incomplete recovery | Markov chains, time-varying reliability |
| Spatially compounding | Spatial synchrony across sites | Spatial copulas, max-stable processes, network models |

### Key Caveat (from Zscheischler et al. 2020)

Most real events fall into more than one class. A monsoon-season flood in Nepal's Terai is simultaneously: preconditioned (saturated soils), multivariate (surge + rainfall), temporally compounding (successive events), and spatially compounding (multiple basins).

## 3. Joint Return Periods and Copula Methods

### The Univariate Assumption and Why It Breaks Down

Everything in traditional flood frequency analysis — fitting GEV, LP3, or Gumbel to annual maxima — assumes one random variable. The design flood has a single exceedance probability.

**The problem:** Most real hydraulic loading is multivariate. A culvert experiences both peak discharge *and* flow duration. A seawall faces wave height *and* storm surge. These variables share common meteorological drivers and are correlated.

### The Core Mathematical Problem

If X and Y each have a 1-in-100-year return period (p = 0.01):

- **Independent:** P(X > x₁₀₀ AND Y > y₁₀₀) = 0.01 × 0.01 = **0.0001** (1-in-10,000-year joint event)
- **Perfectly correlated:** P(X > x₁₀₀ AND Y > y₁₀₀) = **0.01** (same as univariate)
- **Real systems:** Somewhere between — depends on dependence structure

Traditional design provides no way to reason about this intermediate case.

### Design Code Failures

Current civil engineering standards handle this in three unsatisfying ways:

1. **Ignore it entirely** — most river flood standards (HEC guidelines, BS 8533) specify single peak discharge quantile
2. **Prescriptive combination rules** — structural codes (ASCE 7, Eurocode) use load combination factors calibrated empirically with no probabilistic grounding for multi-hazard natural loading
3. **Simplified joint scenarios** — some coastal standards specify discrete combinations (e.g. 10-year surge + 100-year rainfall) by engineering judgement rather than fitted joint distribution

### Research Frontier

Building copula-based joint probability frameworks that give codes a rigorous probabilistic foundation for multi-hazard loading. FEMA's recent updates to coastal flood mapping are beginning to incorporate compound event analysis.

## 4. Salvadori & De Michele (2004) — Copula Framework

### Source: *Water Resources Research*, 2004

### Sklar's Theorem (1959) — The Foundation

> For any joint distribution H with marginals F and G, there exists a copula C such that:
> $$H(x,y) = C(F(x), G(y))$$
> If F and G are continuous, C is unique.

**Practical implication:** You can fit your marginals using whatever distribution best fits your data (GEV for peaks, LP3 for volumes), then choose a copula family that best represents dependence *independently* of those choices. The two fitting steps are decoupled.

---

### Copula Families

#### Archimedean Families

**Gumbel copula:**
$$C(u,v) = \exp\left(-\left[(-\ln u)^\theta + (-\ln v)^\theta\right]^{1/\theta}\right), \quad \theta \ge 1$$
- Upper tail dependence ($\lambda_U > 0$)
- Best for flood peak + volume, surge + rainfall
- Standard for compound flood analysis
- Physical justification: co-driven extreme events (same storm)

**Clayton copula:**
$$C(u,v) = \left[\max\left(u^{-\theta} + v^{-\theta} - 1,\ 0\right)\right]^{-1/\theta}, \quad \theta > 0$$
- Lower tail dependence
- Less common for flood extremes
- Relevant for drought co-occurrence, low-flow analysis

**Frank copula:**
$$C(u,v) = -\frac{1}{\theta}\ln\left[1 + \frac{(e^{-\theta u}-1)(e^{-\theta v}-1)}{e^{-\theta}-1}\right], \quad \theta \ne 0$$
- Symmetric, no tail dependence
- Can handle negative dependence (unlike Gumbel/Clayton)
- Useful baseline comparison

#### Elliptical Families

**Gaussian copula:**
- Symmetric dependence equal at both tails
- Cannot capture upper or lower tail dependence
- Equivalent to bivariate normality — underestimates joint extreme probability
- Convenient but physically unrealistic for flood peaks

**Student-t copula:**
- Symmetric upper AND lower tail dependence
- Controlled by degrees of freedom ν
- More flexible than Gaussian
- Useful for flood peak-duration pairs

---

### Parameter Estimation: Kendall's τ

The rank-based correlation measure τ links to each copula's parameter θ:

| Copula | τ → θ relationship |
|---|---|
| Gumbel | $\theta = 1/(1-\tau)$ |
| Clayton | $\theta = 2\tau/(1-\tau)$ |
| Frank | Numerical inversion of Debye function |

**Workflow:** Compute Kendall's τ from paired flood observations → invert → get θ. This is the **method of moments** approach.

---

### Copula Selection

Use AIC/BIC for model selection, plus tail dependence analysis:

- Empirical upper tail dependence: $\lambda_U = \lim_{u\to 1} P(V>u \mid U>u)$
- Compare to theoretical $\lambda_U$ for each copula family
- Gumbel: $\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta} > 0$
- Gaussian: $\lambda_U = 0$ (no upper tail dependence — often disqualifying for flood analysis)

---

### Practical Workflow in Python

```python
import pyvinecopulib as pv
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import rankdata

# Step 1: transform to uniform pseudo-observations
def to_pseudo_obs(x):
    n = len(x)
    return rankdata(x) / (n + 1)

u = to_pseudo_obs(Q_peak)   # Q = discharge
v = to_pseudo_obs(V_flood)  # V = flood volume
data = np.column_stack([u, v])

# Step 2: fit Gumbel copula via MLE
cop = pv.Bicop(family=pv.BicopFamily.gumbel)
cop.fit(data)
theta = cop.parameters[0][0]
print(f"Fitted θ: {theta:.4f}")

# Step 3: joint return periods
T_design = 100
p_Q = 1 - 1/T_design
p_V = 1 - 1/T_design

p_AND = 1 - p_Q - p_V + cop.cdf(np.array([[p_Q, p_V]]))[0]
p_OR  = 1 - cop.cdf(np.array([[p_Q, p_V]]))[0]

T_AND = 1 / p_AND
T_OR  = 1 / p_OR
print(f"T_AND = {T_AND:.1f} yr, T_OR = {T_OR:.1f} yr")
```

## 5. AND vs OR vs Kendall Return Periods

### Three Definitions

| Return period | Formula | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **AND** | T_AND = μ / P(X>x_T AND Y>y_T) | Both variables must be simultaneously exceeded for failure |
| **OR** | T_OR = μ / P(X>x_T OR Y>y_T) | Either variable exceeding threshold can trigger failure |
| **Kendall** | Based on Kendall distribution K_C(t) | Response is a continuous function of multiple drivers; unique safe/dangerous partition |

Note: T_AND > T_univariate > T_OR (AND gives longest, OR gives shortest joint return period)

---

### When AND is Physically Correct

Failure requires **simultaneous exceedance of multiple thresholds**:

| Structure | Variable pair | Why AND applies |
|---|---|---|
| Large-storage dam spillway | Peak Q + Volume V | Routed pool elevation depends on both; storage attenuates peak-only events |
| Coastal breakwater / seawall | Wave height H + Period T | Run-up requires both H and T above threshold |
| Water supply reservoir | Inflow deficit + demand peak | Supply failure only when both drought AND demand coincide |
| Earthen dam in seismic zone | PGA + antecedent saturation | Liquefaction requires both seismic intensity AND saturation |
| Masonry/adobe structure | Earthquake PGA + prior rainfall | Seismic failure probability elevated only when both conditions are critical |

### When OR is Correct (most flood infrastructure)

Failure can be triggered by **any** of the hazard variables:

| Structure | Reason OR applies |
|---|---|
| River levee / floodwall | Overtopping from high discharge alone, regardless of volume |
| Bridge pier / abutment scour | Scour from peak velocity alone governs failure |
| Run-of-river weir | Minimal storage — peak discharge dominates |
| Urban stormwater network | Pipe surcharge from either high intensity OR long duration |

### When Kendall is Most Appropriate

- **Large dam safety** — routed maximum pool elevation integrates peak, volume, and duration
- **GLOF retention structures** — downstream hazard severity is a joint function of lake volume and peak outflow
- When the failure response is a smooth, continuous function of multiple input variables

### The Routed Return Period (Requena et al. 2013)

Important refinement for dam design: the AND return period in the *input space* (peak, volume) does not directly map to the return period of the *output* (maximum pool elevation).

For design: find the (Q, V) pair on the copula level curve C(F(Q), F(V)) = 1−1/T that **maximises the routed pool elevation**. This requires an optimisation step on top of copula fitting. Directly applicable to Nepal hydropower reservoir design.

---

### Infrastructure Quick Reference Matrix

| Structure type | Variable pair | Failure condition | Correct RP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large-storage dam spillway | Peak Q + Volume V | Routed pool elevation exceeds crest | AND or KENDALL |
| Run-of-river weir / small dam | Peak Q + Volume V | Overtopping from peak alone | OR → univariate |
| River levee / floodwall | Peak Q + surge backwater | Either can overtop independently | OR |
| Bridge pier / abutment | Peak Q + sediment load | Scour from peak Q alone | OR |
| Coastal breakwater | Wave height + Period | Run-up requires both above threshold | AND |
| Water supply reservoir | Inflow deficit + demand | Failure only if both coincide | AND |
| Earthen dam — seismic zone | PGA + saturation | Liquefaction requires both | AND |
| Urban stormwater network | Intensity + duration | Surcharge from either | OR |
| GLOF retention structure | Lake volume + outflow peak | Jointly determined hazard | KENDALL |

## 6. Serinaldi & Kilsby (2015) — Stationarity Is Undead

### Source: *Advances in Water Resources*, 2015

### Context: The Milly et al. (2008) Debate

Milly et al. (2008) — *Science* — argued that stationarity (the assumption of a time-invariant probability density function) is dead under climate change and should be abandoned in water-resource engineering.

This triggered a decade of **non-stationary frequency analysis** — models where GEV parameters (μ, σ, ξ) vary with time or covariates (temperature, CO₂, urbanisation index).

### Serinaldi & Kilsby's Rebuttal

The title is a direct riposte: **stationarity is undead** — you cannot kill it by decree because you have nothing reliably better to replace it with yet.

---

### Two Sources of Uncertainty

**Sampling uncertainty:** Exists even with a perfectly correct model. With 50 years of annual maximum flood data and a fitted GEV, your 100-year quantile carries substantial uncertainty from extrapolating beyond the observed range. This is irreducible for short records.

**Model complexity uncertainty:** Added when switching from stationary (3 parameters: μ, σ, ξ) to non-stationary (4+ parameters: μ(t), σ(t), ξ) GEV. Each additional parameter must be estimated from the same short record, adding its own sampling uncertainty that propagates into the design quantile.

### The Key Insight

These two uncertainties **multiply, not cancel**. You add model complexity to reduce bias (accounting for trend) but simultaneously increase variance (more parameters, same limited data). Serinaldi & Kilsby's empirical finding: for typical engineering record lengths (30–80 years), the complexity-induced uncertainty increase **usually exceeds** the bias reduction from accounting for trend.

**Result:** The non-stationary model's confidence interval on the 100-year flood is so wide that it overlaps almost entirely with the stationary model's — and may be even wider.

### When Non-Stationary FFA Is Defensible

- Record length > ~80 years AND clearly detectable, physically-mechanistic trend
- Trend is attributable to a specific, quantifiable driver (urbanisation index, documented land-use change) not just time as a proxy
- Model selection via AIC/BIC confirms the complexity is justified by the data

### Practical Takeaway for Your Research

Before claiming your model accounts for climate change:
1. Compute confidence intervals on all quantile estimates
2. Compare non-stationary CI width vs stationary CI width
3. If they overlap substantially, the non-stationary model offers no practical improvement
4. Short Nepalese gauge records (often <40 years) make non-stationary FFA particularly vulnerable to this critique

## 7. SPHysics Framework and DualSPHysics

### Why SPH Exists: The Mesh Problem

Grid-based solvers (HEC-RAS, MIKE FLOOD) break down in three scenarios central to civil engineering hazards:
- **Large free-surface deformations** (dam break waves)
- **Fragmentation** (bore hitting a building)
- **Multi-material interaction** (debris in floodwater)

SPH abandons the grid. The fluid is represented as moving **particles** — each carrying mass, position, velocity, pressure, and density. Particles move with the fluid (Lagrangian formulation), so no mesh distortion and the free surface is always explicit.

---

### Governing Equations

The WCSPH (Weakly Compressible SPH) approach solves the Navier-Stokes equations in Lagrangian form:

**Continuity (mass conservation):**
$$\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = -\rho\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$$

**Momentum conservation:**
$$\frac{D\mathbf{v}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{g} + \text{viscosity terms}$$

**Equation of state (closure):**
$$p = c^2(\rho - \rho_0)$$
where:
- ρ₀ = reference density (1000 kg/m³ for water)
- c = artificial speed of sound (≥10× maximum expected flow velocity)
- Density varies ~1% — effectively incompressible, no Poisson solve needed

---

### Kernel Functions

The SPH approximation of continuous fields via discrete particle sums, weighted by kernel W:

$$f(\mathbf{r}_a) \approx \sum_b \frac{m_b}{\rho_b}\, f(\mathbf{r}_b)\, W(|\mathbf{r}_a - \mathbf{r}_b|,\, h)$$

where $h$ = smoothing length, $q = r/h$ = normalised distance.

**Cubic spline (Monaghan 1985):**
- Legacy default; fast to compute
- First derivative → 0 at origin → tensile instability (particle clumping) in violent flows

**Wendland quintic (Wendland 1995) — DualSPHysics default:**
$$W(r,h) = \alpha_D (1-q/2)^4 (2q+1), \qquad 0 \le q \le 2$$

$$\alpha_D = \frac{21}{16\pi h^3} \quad \text{in 3D}$$
- Positive definite, non-zero slope at origin → prevents particle clumping
- Preferred for dam-break, coastal wave impact, debris flow simulations

**Effect of smoothing length h:**
- Larger h → each particle sees more neighbours → smoother but less sharp gradients
- Too small → numerical noise
- Too large → loss of resolution at bore fronts

---

### SPH Particle Discretisation

**Continuity equation (discretised):**
$$\frac{d\rho_a}{dt} = \rho_a \sum_j \frac{m_j}{\rho_j}\, \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \nabla_i W_{ij}$$

**Momentum equation (discretised):**
$$\frac{d\mathbf{v}_a}{dt} = -\sum_b m_b \left(\frac{p_a}{\rho_a^2} + \frac{p_b}{\rho_b^2}\right) \nabla W + \mathbf{g} + \text{viscosity terms}$$

**Position update:**
$$\frac{d\mathbf{r}_a}{dt} = \mathbf{v}_a$$
(particles advect with their own velocity)

This is a system of ODEs — one per particle — integrated forward using a Verlet or Symplectic scheme.

---

### DualSPHysics: Engineering Implementation

**GPU advantage:** A dam-break with 1 million particles simulating 1.5 seconds of physical time:
- 8 CPU processors: ~6 hours
- GTX Titan GPU: ~15 minutes
- RTX 3060 Laptop GPU: expect ~20–30 minutes (good for learning; adequate for research-scale runs)

**Engineering workflow:**

| Stage | Tool | Key parameters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Geometry | DesignSPHysics / FreeCAD + BathymetryTool | DEM → STL surface |
| 2. Case setup | GenCase → XML | Particle spacing dp, kernel, viscosity model, boundary conditions |
| 3. Simulation | DualSPHysics executable | WCSPH time loop, Courant condition |
| 4. Post-processing | PartVTK, MeasureTool, IsoSurface | VTK → ParaView, gauge time series |
| 5. Engineering output | ParaView + analysis scripts | Impact pressure, inundation depth, velocity, forces |

**Key parameter: particle spacing dp**
- Halving dp → 8× more particles (3D) → ~16× compute time
- Engineering validation studies: dp = 0.02–0.05m for flume-scale
- Real terrain: dp = 0.5–2m depending on GPU memory

**Civil engineering applications:**
- Dam-break flood wave propagation and validation
- Coastal wave impact on seawalls and breakwaters
- Debris flow and impact forces on check dams (SPH-DEM coupling)
- Tsunami run-up and urban inundation

**Key limitation:** Computational cost prevents kilometre-scale SPH at sub-metre resolution without cluster computing. This is precisely why the SPH → AI surrogate approach is valuable: SPH generates high-fidelity training data at small scale; AI emulates the physics at full scale.

theta = cop.parameters[0][0]

## 11. Key Equations Reference

### Copula Theory

**Sklar's theorem:**
$$H(x,y) = C(F(x), G(y))$$

**Gumbel copula:**
$$C(u,v) = \exp\left(-\left[(-\ln u)^\theta + (-\ln v)^\theta\right]^{1/\theta}\right)$$

$$\tau = 1 - \frac{1}{\theta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \frac{1}{1-\tau}$$

**Clayton copula:**
$$C(u,v) = \left[u^{-\theta} + v^{-\theta} - 1\right]^{-1/\theta}$$

$$\tau = \frac{\theta}{\theta+2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \frac{2\tau}{1-\tau}$$

**Joint return periods:**
$$T_{AND} = \frac{\mu}{P(X>x_T \text{ and } Y>y_T)} = \frac{\mu}{1 - F(x) - G(y) + C(F(x),G(y))}$$

$$T_{OR} = \frac{\mu}{P(X>x_T \text{ or } Y>y_T)} = \frac{\mu}{1 - C(F(x),G(y))}$$

**Upper tail dependence:**
$$\lambda_U = \lim_{u\to 1} P(V>u \mid U>u) = 2 - 2^{1/\theta} \quad \text{(Gumbel)}$$

$$\lambda_U = 0 \quad \text{(Gaussian)}$$

### SPH Governing Equations

**Continuity (Lagrangian):**
$$\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = -\rho\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$$

**Momentum:**
$$\frac{D\mathbf{v}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{g} + \text{viscosity}$$

**Equation of state (WCSPH):**
$$p = c^2(\rho - \rho_0)$$

**Wendland quintic kernel:**
$$W(r,h) = \frac{21}{16\pi h^3}(1-q/2)^4(2q+1), \qquad 0 \le q \le 2$$

$$q = r/h$$

**SPH discretised continuity:**
$$\frac{d\rho_a}{dt} = \rho_a \sum_j \frac{m_j}{\rho_j}\, \mathbf{v}_{ij} \cdot \nabla_i W_{ij}$$

**SPH discretised momentum:**
$$\frac{d\mathbf{v}_a}{dt} = -\sum_b m_b \left(\frac{p_a}{\rho_a^2}+\frac{p_b}{\rho_b^2}\right)\nabla W + \mathbf{g}$$
