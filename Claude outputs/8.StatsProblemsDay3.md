*Companion to `5.3.StatsDay3_ExtremeValueTheory.md` and the interactive page
`GEV_Extreme_Value_interactive.html`. Topic: maximum likelihood estimation, why block maxima need

shape parameter ξ and its three tail regimes, and why ξ's uncertainty differs so much between
Kathmandu and Surkhet — the bridge to the copula workflow's margin.*

## Section 0 — Pre-questions (answer from memory, before anything else)

Write answers to all six now. No notes, no calculator, no scrolling. Ten minutes maximum. You are
*expected* to get some wrong — that is what makes the rest of the set work.

1. Why does MLE pick p = 0.8 for 8 heads out of 10 flips, rather than 0.5?
2. Why can't you fit a GEV directly to the raw daily rainfall series?
3. Name the three GEV tail regimes and what ξ's sign tells you about each.
4. Kathmandu and Surkhet both have 41 years of record. Why is Surkhet's ξ so much less certain?
5. In one sentence each: what does the Central Limit Theorem govern, and what does the
   Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem govern?
6. A 95% confidence interval for ξ is (0.08, 3.34). What does that interval actually promise —
   and what common misreading does it invite?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Model answers — open only after writing all six</b></summary>

1. Because 0.8 is the value of p under which the *observed* outcome — exactly 8 heads, 2 tails —
   is the most probable. MLE does not ask "what's the fairest guess"; it asks "under which
   candidate is this exact data least surprising."
2. A raw daily reading is "block size 1" — not a maximum of anything. FTG is a theorem about
   *maxima of blocks*, so fitting GEV to unblocked daily data isn't imprecise, it's outside what
   the theorem promises at all — a category error, not a shortcut.
3. Gumbel (ξ = 0): exponentially fast tail decay, no hard ceiling. Fréchet (ξ > 0): power-law
   decay, slow, no ceiling — the heavy-tail regime. Weibull (ξ < 0): a genuine finite upper bound
   the variable can mathematically never cross.
4. Because ξ is estimated almost entirely from the top few order statistics. Surkhet has one year
   (423.1 mm) sitting far above its next-highest (281.2 mm, a 141.9 mm gap); Kathmandu's top values
   decrease smoothly (177.0 vs 124.4, a 52.6 mm gap). Same record length, very different amount of
   leverage sitting on one point.
5. CLT: the **average** of many values converges to Normal, regardless of the parent distribution.
   FTG: the **maximum** of many values converges to GEV, regardless of the parent distribution.
   Same shape of result, different summary statistic.
6. It promises that if you repeated the entire data-collection-and-refit process many times, about
   95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true ξ — a statement about the *procedure*, not
   a probability about this one fixed interval. The common misreading is "there's a 95% chance the
   true ξ is in (0.08, 3.34)", which treats the fixed true parameter as if it were the random
   quantity, when in frequentist terms it's the interval that's random.

Score yourself out of 6. Whatever you missed, that section of the set is the one to slow down on.
</details>

## Set A — Foundations (recognition and reasoning, no calculator)

### A1 — worked example (read this one; do not skip it)

*Three stations' fitted ξ: Kathmandu −0.003, Biratnagar −0.185, Surkhet +0.398. Classify each by
regime, and say what each implies for a "worst case ever recorded" argument.*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Worked solution</b></summary>

![Three GEV density curves at xi=-0.185 (Weibull, bounded, finite endpoint marked), xi=-0.003 (Gumbel, exponential tail, no ceiling) and xi=+0.398 (Frechet, heavy power-law tail, no ceiling) sharing the same mu=0, sigma=1 standardisation](images/day3-xi-regimes.png)

- **Kathmandu (ξ = −0.003), essentially Gumbel.** Tail decays exponentially fast. No hard ceiling
  in the mathematical sense, but very large values become rare very quickly — the 1000-year level
  (192.4 mm) is barely above the 100-year level (153.1 mm).
- **Biratnagar (ξ = −0.185), Weibull regime.** A real, finite upper endpoint exists
  (μ − σ/ξ = 118.33 − 38.66/(−0.185) ≈ 327 mm) — the fitted model says rainfall mathematically
  cannot exceed roughly that value. Note this is a *model* implication, not a law of nature; a
  negative-ξ fit can still be wrong about where the true physical ceiling sits.
- **Surkhet (ξ = +0.398), Fréchet regime.** No ceiling at all, and the tail is heavy — the
  1000-year level (1028.3 mm) is more than double the 100-year level (430.0 mm). This is the
  station where "the worst year we've seen" is least informative about what's physically possible.

The transferable move: the *sign* of ξ answers "is there a ceiling," and the *magnitude* answers
"how fast does probability fall away as you approach it (or don't)." Don't stop at "positive means
heavy-tailed" — say what that implies for extrapolation past the record.
</details>

### A2
Pokhara has ξ = −0.061, Simara has ξ = −0.101. Both are technically "Weibull regime." Is that
classification doing useful work here, or is something being lost by only reporting the sign?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Something is lost. Both are close enough to ξ = 0 that their behaviour over any *practically
relevant* range of return periods looks almost indistinguishable from Gumbel — the finite endpoint
these fits imply is far out past anything a 41-year record can pin down with confidence (recall the
Kathmandu bootstrap CI, (−0.30, 0.26), straddles zero entirely). Reporting only the regime name
without the magnitude — or without the uncertainty on ξ — overstates how confidently "bounded" or
"unbounded" the station actually is. 🔭 **[FUTURE]** This is exactly why a bootstrap CI on ξ, not
just the point estimate, has to travel with any regime classification used downstream.
</details>

### A3
*"Our 41-year record's largest event at Surkhet is 423.1 mm, so that's roughly the 40-year flood."*
One thing right, one thing wrong.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Right:** the sample maximum of an n-year record is a reasonable rough estimate of the event with
return period near n years (plotting-position intuition — the largest of 41 draws has an expected
non-exceedance probability around 41/42).

**Wrong:** treating it as *the* number, rather than one noisy draw from a distribution whose spread
is itself uncertain — especially at Surkhet, where the fitted GEV's own 10-year level is 188.4 mm,
well below the observed 423.1 mm. That gap is a flag, not a contradiction: it says the single
largest year was an unusually extreme draw even under the fitted heavy-tailed model, which is
precisely the situation (a lone dominant point) discussed in Panel 5 of the interactive page.
</details>

### A4
*"Fitting a Normal distribution to the annual maxima should be fine, since annual maxima are
already 'the extreme values' — the extra machinery of GEV is for the raw daily series."*
What's wrong with this reasoning?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

It reverses which series needs which treatment. GEV is specifically the correct family **for**
block maxima (that's what FTG proves converges to it); a Normal fit to annual maxima repeats
exactly the Day 1 mistake — assuming a symmetric, thin-tailed shape for a right-skewed quantity —
one level up. Set C below computes precisely how much a Normal fit to Kathmandu's and Surkhet's own
annual maxima understates the far tail, even though annual maxima already are "the extreme values."
"Already extreme" describes the *data*, not the *shape of distribution that data follows* — those
are separate questions.
</details>

### A5
For a Fréchet-regime station (ξ > 0), does a larger ξ mean the 100-year and 1000-year return levels
get *closer together* or *farther apart*, relative to a station with smaller positive ξ? Use the
Surkhet vs Biratnagar-were-it-positive intuition, or reason from the tail-decay-speed description.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Farther apart.** Larger ξ means slower (heavier) tail decay, so probability keeps a meaningfully
larger footprint further out — pushing the T=1000 quantile up much faster than the T=100 quantile
as ξ grows. Concretely: Surkhet's ratio (1000-yr / 100-yr level) is 1028.3/430.0 ≈ 2.39; a
Gumbel-regime station like Kathmandu has ratio 192.4/153.1 ≈ 1.26. The heavier the tail, the more
the far-out return periods diverge from the near ones — which is exactly why "report the spread
across return periods," not a single design number, matters more as ξ grows.
</details>

## Set B — Mechanics (worked, then scaffolded)

### B1 — worked example (read this one; do not skip it)

*Ten coin flips, 8 heads. Compute the likelihood L(p) = C(10,8)·p⁸·(1−p)² at p = 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9
and confirm which wins.*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Worked solution</b></summary>

![Binomial likelihood curve for 8 heads out of 10 flips, peaking at p=0.8, with points marked at p=0.5, 0.7, 0.8 and 0.9](images/day3-mle-coinflip.png)

C(10,8) = 45.

| p | p⁸ | (1−p)² | L(p) = 45·p⁸·(1−p)² |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.00391 | 0.25 | **0.0439** |
| 0.7 | 0.05765 | 0.09 | **0.2335** |
| 0.8 | 0.16777 | 0.04 | **0.3020** |
| 0.9 | 0.43047 | 0.01 | **0.1937** |

p = 0.8 wins — about 6.9× more likely than p = 0.5, and higher than both its neighbours 0.7 and
0.9, consistent with it being the peak of a smooth curve rather than a coincidence of the four
points sampled. Calculus confirms p̂ = k/n = 0.8 exactly maximises L(p) over *all* candidates in
[0,1], not just these four.
</details>

### B2
Fit a Normal(μ, σ=1) to the three points {2, 4, 6} at μ = 3 and μ = 4 (heights and product). Which
wins, and by what factor? (These numbers also drive Panel 2 of the interactive page — check your
arithmetic against it.)

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

![Two Normal density curves centred at mu=3 and mu=4, each with three marked heights at x=2,4,6, alongside the likelihood-vs-mu curve peaking at mu=4](images/day3-mle-continuous.png)

At μ=3: heights 0.2420, 0.2420, 0.00443 → L = **0.000259**.
At μ=4: heights 0.0540, 0.3989, 0.0540 → L = **0.001163**.

μ = 4 wins, by a factor of 0.001163/0.000259 ≈ **4.5×**. Note the asymmetry in *why*: at μ=3 no
single height is catastrophically bad (0.00443 is small but not zero) — it's the product of two
merely-good heights and one poor one that loses out to μ=4's more balanced three heights. The
sample mean, 4, is the exact maximiser — provable by setting d(log L)/dμ = 0, not just the winner
among these two guesses.
</details>

### B3
Design life n = 25 years, design return period T = 100 years. Compute the risk R — the probability
of at least one exceedance somewhere in that 25-year design life — using R = 1 − (1 − 1/T)ⁿ.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

R = 1 − (1 − 1/100)²⁵ = 1 − 0.99²⁵ = 1 − 0.7778 = **0.2222**, i.e. about **22%**.

Worth sitting with: a "1-in-100-year" design standard still carries better than a 1-in-5 chance of
being exceeded at least once across a typical 25-year structure lifetime. "100-year flood" reads as
reassuring; 22% does not, and they are the same statement.
</details>

### B4
Using Kathmandu's fitted parameters (ξ = −0.003, μ = 74.07, σ = 17.29), the 100-year return level is
given as 153.1 mm above. Sanity-check this is at least the right order of magnitude by confirming it
sits above μ and grows with σ — you are not asked to reproduce the exact GEV quantile formula by
hand, only to check the fitted number is not obviously broken.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

153.1 mm is (153.1 − 74.07)/17.29 ≈ **4.57 σ above** the location parameter μ — a large, "rare
event" distance in the right direction and the right order of magnitude for a 1-in-100-year event
built from a 41-year record (spanning roughly 2.4× the record length in return period). If a fitted
100-year level had come out *below* μ, or within 1σ of it, that would be a red flag worth
re-checking the fit for (wrong sign convention on ξ is the most common such bug — scipy's `c` is
**minus** ξ, and it is easy to pass the wrong one to `.ppf()`).
</details>

### B5 — scaffolded PWM hand calculation
*A toy 5-year annual-maxima record (illustrative, not a real station): sorted values
{40, 55, 60, 70, 100}. The probability-weighted moment b₀ (just the sample mean) and b₁ are given:
b₀ = 65.000, b₁ = 39.250. Compute b₂ using
b₂ = (1/n)·Σᵢ [(i−1)(i−2)/((n−1)(n−2))]·x₍ᵢ₎ for i = 1..5, then get λ₁, λ₂, λ₃ and τ₃.*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Weights (i−1)(i−2)/((n−1)(n−2)) for i=1..5, n=5: i=1→0, i=2→0, i=3→(2)(1)/(4)(3)=1⁄6,
i=4→(3)(2)/12=1⁄2, i=5→(4)(3)/12=1.

b₂ = (1⁄5)·[0·40 + 0·55 + (1⁄6)·60 + (1⁄2)·70 + 1·100] = (1⁄5)·[10 + 35 + 100] = (1⁄5)·145 =
**29.000**.

λ₁ = b₀ = **65.000**
λ₂ = 2b₁ − b₀ = 2(39.250) − 65.000 = **13.500**
λ₃ = 6b₂ − 6b₁ + b₀ = 6(29.000) − 6(39.250) + 65.000 = 174.000 − 235.500 + 65.000 = **3.500**
τ₃ = λ₃/λ₂ = 3.500/13.500 = **0.2593**

(Verified against `lmoments3.lmom_ratios`: l1=65.0, l2=13.5, τ₃=0.2593 — exact match.)
</details>

### B6
Continuing B5: using Hosking's approximation c = 2/(3+τ₃) − ln2/ln3 and ξ ≈ 7.8590c + 2.9554c²,
compute ξ for the toy record.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

c = 2/(3+0.2593) − ln2/ln3 = 2÷3.2593 − 0.6931/1.0986 = 0.6137 − 0.6309 = **−0.0173**

ξ ≈ 7.8590(−0.0173) + 2.9554(−0.0173)² = −0.1358 + 0.00088 = **−0.1350**

A mildly bounded (Weibull-regime) shape for this 5-point toy record. With n = 5 this is far too
short a record to trust as a real estimate — the exercise is the arithmetic chain, not the number.
</details>

## Set C — The core argument (comparison and inference)

### C1
Fit a Normal distribution to Kathmandu's own 41 annual maxima directly (mean 83.84 mm, sd 22.83 mm
— a legitimate, if wrong, thing to try). At what return period does the Normal model say
Kathmandu's *true* GEV 100-year level (153.1 mm) actually sits?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

The Normal model puts 153.1 mm at roughly a **1-in-831-year** event — understating a true
100-year event's rarity by about **8.3×**. This is Day 1's whole argument, replayed one level up:
the annual maxima themselves are right-skewed (sample skewness 1.63), so fitting a symmetric
Normal to *them* repeats exactly the mistake Day 1 diagnosed for raw discharge, even though these
are already "the extreme values."
</details>

### C2
Repeat C1 for Surkhet (Normal fit: mean 124.94 mm, sd 63.59 mm; true GEV 1000-year level 1028.3
mm). Compute how rare the Normal model thinks that value is, and comment on why the number is
absurd rather than merely large.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

The Normal model places 1028.3 mm at roughly a **1-in-2.4×10⁴⁵-year** event (computed exactly via
`scipy.stats.norm.sf`) — a number with no physical meaning whatsoever; it is smaller than the age
of the universe raised to several powers. The absurdity is diagnostic, not decorative: it is what
happens when a thin-tailed model is extrapolated into a region governed by a fundamentally
different tail-decay rate (Surkhet's sample skewness is 2.95, even more skewed than Kathmandu's).
The GEV number, 1028.3 mm, is itself uncertain — but it is the right *family* of model to be
uncertain within; the Normal number is confidently wrong.
</details>

### C3
Why does taking **annual** blocks (not monthly) matter for whether the daily zero-atom reaches the
GEV fit, specifically for a monsoon climate like Nepal's?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

A block's maximum is zero only if **every single day in that block** was dry. For an annual block
in monsoon Nepal, that would require a complete year without one wet day — not observed in the
41-year record for any of the 5 stations, and not a realistic climatological outcome (the monsoon
reliably delivers wet days June–September). For a **monthly** block, a fully dry month is genuinely
possible in the dry season (roughly Nov–Feb) — reintroducing a small but real atom at exactly zero
into the monthly-maxima series, which annual blocks avoid. This is why block size is not just a
bias/variance dial (A2/Day 3 notes) — it also determines whether the atom-at-zero problem that
motivated the whole session even applies.
</details>

### C4
Kathmandu's MLE ξ 95% bootstrap CI is (−0.30, 0.26), width 0.56. Surkhet's is (0.08, 3.34), width
3.25 — 5.8× wider. Both records are 41 years. Explain the mechanism, not just the fact.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

ξ is estimated almost entirely from the top few order statistics, not all 41 points with equal
weight. In the parametric bootstrap, each of the 500 resamples draws a fresh synthetic 41-year
series from the *fitted* model and refits. For Kathmandu, the top values decrease smoothly, so
resamples produce broadly similar "shape" of upper tail each time — the refits cluster. For
Surkhet, the fitted model itself has ξ = 0.398 (Fréchet, heavy-tailed), so its *own* resampling
distribution occasionally throws an extreme value even more extreme than 423.1 mm, and sometimes
none nearly as extreme — the refit ξ swings hard in both directions across resamples. The
gap-to-next-highest in the *observed* record (52.6 mm at Kathmandu vs 141.9 mm at Surkhet) is a
symptom of the same underlying heavy-tailedness that also drives the bootstrap's instability — not
two separate facts.
</details>

### C5
The 5-station table below gives MLE ξ and L-moments ξ side by side. Which station shows the
*largest* disagreement between the two methods, and does that ranking match the ranking of |ξ|
itself?

| Station | MLE ξ | L-moments ξ | \|difference\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kathmandu | −0.003 | −0.002 | 0.001 |
| Pokhara | −0.061 | +0.004 | 0.065 |
| Simara | −0.101 | −0.090 | 0.011 |
| Biratnagar | −0.185 | −0.153 | 0.032 |
| Surkhet | +0.398 | +0.350 | 0.048 |

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Pokhara** shows the largest disagreement (0.065) — not Surkhet (0.048), even though Surkhet has
the largest |ξ| by far. This is worth sitting with precisely because it *doesn't* match the naive
expectation "biggest ξ, biggest disagreement." Pokhara's MLE and L-moments fits even disagree on
**sign** (−0.061 vs +0.004) — a case where the two estimators can't even agree which regime the
station is in, despite both ξ values being individually small and unremarkable-looking. The lesson:
raw |ξ| is not itself a reliable proxy for estimation instability; check the actual disagreement (or
better, the bootstrap CI) rather than assuming "small ξ" means "well-determined ξ."
</details>

### C6
Given ξ ranges from −0.185 to +0.398 across just 5 stations in one country, what specifically goes
wrong if the copula workflow used one shared national ξ for all stations' margins?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Using a shared ξ would force every station onto the same tail regime — e.g. a Gumbel-ish national
ξ ≈ −0.03 (roughly the mean of the 5) would badly *understate* Surkhet's true heavy-tailed risk
(Fréchet, ξ ≈ +0.40) while mildly overstating Biratnagar's (Weibull, ξ ≈ −0.19, which does have a
real finite endpoint the shared value would miss). Since λ_U (the copula's tail dependence
parameter, Day 2) is invariant to the margins under Sklar's theorem, a wrong margin doesn't corrupt
the *dependence* structure directly — but it does corrupt every design value converted back to real
units (mm, m³/s) at the end of the pipeline, station by station, in opposite directions depending
on each station's true ξ. The margin has to stay station-specific, or be pooled deliberately via
regional frequency analysis — not averaged.
</details>

## Set D — CCH bridge (why it matters for this project)

### D1
In your own words: what specific open question, standing since 2026-07-25, does having a real
fitted GEV margin for 5 Nepal stations resolve — and what does it *not* resolve?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Resolves:** the "real data vs. pseudo-observations" question — the copula workflow now has an
actual fitted univariate margin (station-level annual-maximum rainfall) rather than needing to
proceed on synthetic or placeholder data.

**Does not resolve:** the "need a real flood peak–volume dataset" blocker. Rainfall annual maxima
is a single-variable margin; the copula stage needs a genuine bivariate pair (e.g. flood peak and
flood volume from the same event), which requires a separate rainfall→discharge modelling step this
work has not touched. A margin existing is necessary but not sufficient for the bivariate workflow.
</details>

### D2
Surkhet's 1000-year return level (1028.3 mm) is over 5× Kathmandu's (192.4 mm). If these two
stations were both inputs to a regional joint-hazard assessment using a shared copula family, what
would *not* need to change between them, and what *would*?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

**Would not need to change:** the copula family and its dependence parameter, if the two stations'
relationship to a *shared* driver (e.g. the same monsoon system) has similar tail dependence — that
is a property of the copula, and by Sklar's theorem it is separable from each station's own margin.

**Would need to change:** each station's marginal transform — the rank/PIT step (Day 2) that
converts real rainfall depths to the uniform (0,1) scale the copula operates on has to use each
station's *own* fitted GEV (or its empirical ranks), not a shared one. Get this wrong and a
correctly-fitted copula still produces a wrong design value once converted back to real mm, because
the error was injected at the margin step, not the dependence step (this is the exact correction
recorded against the Day 2 CCH bridge in the 2026-07-29 log).
</details>

### D3
The L-moments open thread (re-fit ξ for all 5 stations, especially Surkhet, and compare to MLE) is
explicitly flagged as "for the user to run, not for them." Given the leave-one-out finding in Panel
5 of the interactive page — that L-moments swung by *more* than MLE when Surkhet's outlier year was
removed — what should that re-fit actually be checked against, if not "does L-moments move less
than MLE"?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

The bootstrap CI **width**, not a single leave-one-out deletion. The theoretical claim for
L-moments (a bounded influence function, so no single value can dominate through the same
cube-power amplification MLE-adjacent moment methods are prone to) is a statement about how the
estimate behaves under *resampling* — many synthetic 41-year records, not the removal of one
specific point from the one real record. The correct next check is: run the same 500-resample
parametric bootstrap already used for MLE ξ, but refit with L-moments at each resample, and compare
the resulting CI width to MLE's 3.25 at Surkhet. That is a different, and more informative, test
than the leave-one-out numbers already shown.
</details>

### D4
Why does a wide bootstrap CI on ξ (Surkhet: (0.08, 3.34)) matter for a downstream design decision,
beyond just "the number is uncertain"?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Because ξ enters the return-level formula nonlinearly, and Set A5 already showed larger ξ pushes
far-out return periods disproportionately higher. A CI that wide on ξ translates into an enormous
range of plausible 1000-year design values at Surkhet — not a narrow band around 1028.3 mm. Quoting
a single design number from the point estimate, without carrying the uncertainty through to the
design-value stage, would misrepresent how settled the Surkhet design flood actually is — precisely
the project's standing "carry 2–3 candidates through and report the spread, don't collapse to one
number" convention (Next Action 7 in the project status file), here shown to matter within a single
station's margin fitting, not only across candidate copula families.
</details>

## Set E — Hard (synthesis and derivation)

### E1
Derive, from calculus, that the sample mean maximises the log-likelihood of a Normal(μ, σ=1) fit to
data x₁,...,xₙ — i.e. reproduce the argument behind B2's result that μ=4 (not 3 or 5) is the *exact*
maximiser for {2,4,6}, not just the best of the candidates tried.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

log L(μ) = Σᵢ log f(xᵢ;μ) = Σᵢ [−½log(2π) − ½(xᵢ−μ)²] = const − ½Σᵢ(xᵢ−μ)².

d(log L)/dμ = Σᵢ(xᵢ−μ) = (Σxᵢ) − nμ. Setting this to zero: μ̂ = (Σxᵢ)/n — the sample mean, exactly.
The second derivative is −n < 0, confirming a maximum (not a minimum or saddle). For {2,4,6}:
μ̂ = (2+4+6)/3 = 4, matching B2's numerical result — and proving it holds against *every* candidate
μ, not only 3, 4 and 5.
</details>

### E2
Why is maximising log L(μ) equivalent to maximising L(μ) itself — and why does virtually every real
MLE implementation (including what `scipy.stats.genextreme.fit` does internally) work with the log
rather than the raw product?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

log is a strictly increasing function, so it preserves the location of maxima: if L(μ*) ≥ L(μ) for
all μ, then log L(μ*) ≥ log L(μ) for all μ, and vice versa. The switch to log is purely a
numerical-stability move: the raw likelihood is a *product* of many densities (41 of them, for a
station fit), each typically well below 1 — the product underflows to numerically indistinguishable
from zero long before an optimiser can compare candidates usefully. The log turns that product into
a *sum*, which stays in a well-behaved numerical range regardless of sample size.
</details>

### E3
Show that the standard GEV survival function collapses to the Gumbel form as ξ → 0, i.e. that the
ξ ≠ 0 formula and the ξ = 0 formula are not two unrelated cases but a single continuous family.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

For ξ ≠ 0: F(x) = exp[−(1+ξx)^(−1/ξ)]. Write (1+ξx)^(−1/ξ) = exp[−(1/ξ)·ln(1+ξx)]. As ξ→0,
ln(1+ξx) ≈ ξx − (ξx)²/2 + ..., so −(1/ξ)·ln(1+ξx) ≈ −x + ξx²/2 − ... → −x. Substituting back:
(1+ξx)^(−1/ξ) → exp(−x), so F(x) → exp[−exp(−x)] — exactly the Gumbel CDF used directly in Panel 4
and Panel 1's `gevPdf` code for |ξ|<1e-6. The three regimes are not three different formulas
stitched together; ξ=0 is a removable-singularity limit of the same expression, which is also why
the interactive page's code has a single `if(Math.abs(xi)<1e-6)` branch rather than three.
</details>

### E4
Using R = 1 − (1 − 1/T)ⁿ, solve for the return period T needed so that a 50-year design life has
risk R < 5%.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

0.05 = 1 − (1 − 1/T)⁵⁰ → (1 − 1/T)⁵⁰ = 0.95 → 1 − 1/T = 0.95^(1/50) = 0.998976 →
1/T = 0.001024 → **T ≈ 977 years**.

Sanity check against B3's direction of travel: B3 found T=100, n=25 gives a surprisingly high 22%
risk; here, pushing the acceptable risk down to 5% over a *longer* 50-year life demands a design
return period nearly 1000 years — an order of magnitude beyond what "100-year flood" language
usually connotes to a non-specialist.
</details>

### E5
Cold (no scaffolding this time): a different toy 5-year record has τ₃ = 0.10. Compute ξ via
Hosking's approximation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

c = 2/(3+0.10) − ln2/ln3 = 2÷3.10 − 0.6931/1.0986 = 0.6452 − 0.6309 = **0.0143**

ξ ≈ 7.8590(0.0143) + 2.9554(0.0143)² = 0.1124 + 0.00060 = **0.1130**

A mild Fréchet shape — much closer to Gumbel than B6's toy record (ξ ≈ −0.135), consistent with
τ₃ closer to 0 meaning less skew in the sorted-value weighting that drives ξ's estimate.
</details>

### E6
Structurally — not just "one is cubed and one isn't" — why does deleting a single extreme order
statistic move an MLE-type estimator differently from an L-moments estimator? Connect this to Day
1's skewness-coefficient argument.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

MLE for ξ solves a nonlinear optimisation over the full likelihood surface; deleting one point
changes *every term* in that optimisation indirectly, because the optimiser re-balances against
what's left, and the objective's curvature near the optimum is itself shaped disproportionately by
extreme points (echoing Day 1: a deviation twice as large contributes a superlinearly larger amount
to a power-based objective). L-moments, by construction, are **linear** combinations of the sorted
values with fixed, precomputed weights (Set B) — deleting one point removes exactly its own weighted
contribution and nothing more, no re-balancing. That linearity is why L-moments' *influence
function* (the mathematical object describing how much one observation can move the estimate) stays
bounded for any single value, however extreme — but the leave-one-out numbers (Panel 5, Set G5)
show that "bounded influence" is not the same claim as "moves less under any specific test you
happen to run." A bounded influence function limits worst-case sensitivity to a *replaced* value's
magnitude; it says nothing about the size of the *jump* from including vs. entirely deleting the
top point of a small sample, which is a different, cruder perturbation.
</details>

## Set G — Misconception clinic

### G1
*"MLE picked p̂ = 0.8 from 8 heads out of 10 flips, so if I flip 3 more times, I should expect
exactly 2.4 heads."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

p̂ = 0.8 is a point estimate of the coin's long-run bias, not a guarantee about any specific future
sequence. The *expected* number of heads in 3 more flips is 3 × 0.8 = 2.4, but expectation is not a
prediction of what will happen — you cannot observe 2.4 heads, only 0, 1, 2 or 3. Confusing a
fitted parameter with a forecast of the next realisation is the same category error as reading a
95% CI as a probability statement about one fixed interval (G3, and Section 0 Q6).
</details>

### G2
*"GEV needs its own theorem because rainfall data isn't Normally distributed to begin with."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Wrong reason, even though the conclusion (GEV is needed) is right. FTG applies *regardless* of the
parent distribution's shape — that is the entire content of the theorem, exactly parallel to how
CLT applies to averages regardless of the parent shape. GEV isn't needed because rainfall is
non-Normal; it would be needed even if daily rainfall somehow *were* exactly Normal, because the
theorem being invoked is about what happens to **maxima of blocks**, a completely different
question from what shape the raw data follows. This is the single most consequential misconception
flagged in the Day 3 notes (Check 3) — conflating CLT's territory (averages) with FTG's (maxima).
</details>

### G3
*"Surkhet's ξ has a 95% confidence interval of (0.08, 3.34), so there's a 95% chance the true ξ is
between those numbers."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

This is the standard frequentist-CI misreading (Section 0, Q6). The true ξ is a fixed, unknown
number — it either is or isn't in (0.08, 3.34), with no meaningful "chance" attached to it once the
data are in hand. What has a 95% probability is the *procedure*: if you repeated the whole
41-year-record-and-refit exercise many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain
the true ξ. It's a subtle distinction that rarely changes what you'd *do* differently, but it does
change what you're licensed to claim in a methods section.
</details>

### G4
*"Kathmandu's ξ ≈ 0 puts it in the Gumbel regime, so Kathmandu does have a ceiling on possible
rainfall — just a very high one, unlike Surkhet which has none."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

False, and worth being precise about why. Gumbel (ξ = 0) has **no finite upper endpoint at all** —
its support is unbounded above, exactly like Fréchet. What's true is that Gumbel's tail decays
*fast* (exponentially), so extremely large values become rare very quickly — but "rare" is not
"impossible." Only the **Weibull** regime (ξ < 0, e.g. Biratnagar's ξ = −0.185) produces a genuine
mathematical ceiling. Conflating "decays fast" with "is bounded" collapses two of the three regimes
into one and erases the actual distinction ξ's sign is there to capture.
</details>

### G5
*"L-moments are more stable than MLE, so for any GEV fit you should just always trust the
L-moments ξ over the MLE ξ."*

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Overreach from a real result. L-moments' advantage is specifically about resampling variance (the
bootstrap CI) for estimators built from linear combinations of order statistics — not a guarantee
that L-moments is closer to the truth in every individual fit, or even less sensitive to every kind
of perturbation. The leave-one-out table (Set D3, interactive Panel 5) shows L-moments' ξ actually
swung by *more* than MLE's when Surkhet's and Kathmandu's own top years were individually deleted
(0.168 vs 0.134 at Surkhet; 0.208 vs 0.216 at Kathmandu — comparable, not obviously smaller). "More
stable under resampling" and "always closer to correct" are different claims, and only the first one
currently has evidence behind it here.
</details>

## Set I — Interleaved mixed set (no labels, no order)

*Do these in one sitting, cold, at least a day after the rest. They are deliberately unsorted —
part of the task is recognising which idea each one needs.*

**I1.** Design life n = 10 years, acceptable risk R = 10%. Solve for the required return period T.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

0.10 = 1 − (1−1/T)¹⁰ → (1−1/T)¹⁰ = 0.90 → 1−1/T = 0.90^0.1 = 0.98953 → 1/T = 0.01047 →
**T ≈ 95.5 years**. Notice how close this sits to "T=100" for a *short* 10-year design life — the
1-in-100 heuristic quietly assumes a design life in this range, which is worth checking explicitly
rather than assuming, per B3 and E4.
</details>

**I2.** A station's fitted ξ = +0.15, with a 95% bootstrap CI of (−0.05, 0.40). Classify its regime,
and say whether the CI leaves the regime classification in real doubt.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Point estimate: **Fréchet** (ξ > 0). But the CI spans zero and reaches into mildly negative
territory — so at the 95% level, the data cannot rule out Gumbel, or even a very mild Weibull
regime. The honest statement is "point estimate suggests Fréchet, but the regime itself is not
well-determined at this record length" — not a confident "this station is Fréchet."
</details>

**I3.** Why does fitting GEV to raw daily data fail differently from fitting a Normal to skewed
discharge (Day 1)? Both are "wrong model" situations — what's the structural difference?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Day 1's Normal-on-skewed-discharge is a **misspecification within scope** — a Normal distribution
is a legitimate thing to fit to that kind of data, it's just the wrong shape, and a goodness-of-fit
test can meaningfully say so. GEV-on-raw-daily-data is **outside the theorem's scope entirely** —
FTG makes a promise about maxima of blocks, and raw daily readings aren't maxima of anything (block
size 1). It isn't a bad fit to a valid question; it's fitting the right-family-for-a-different
question to data the theorem never addressed. One is an accuracy problem; the other is a category
error, and no amount of additional daily data fixes it — only extracting proper block maxima does.
</details>

**I4.** Recall Day 1's finding that a Normal model understates a real river's threshold exceedance
by orders of magnitude at the far tail. Using C2's number, is Day 3's Normal-vs-GEV mismatch at
Surkhet worse or comparable in *kind*, even though the numbers involved are different?

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

Comparable in *kind*, and arguably more extreme in *degree* here: Day 1's log-normal-vs-Normal
example understated exceedance by factors growing into the thousands at extreme thresholds; C2's
Normal-vs-GEV comparison at Surkhet's 1000-year level produced a "return period" with over 40
digits — not because Day 3's mechanism is fundamentally different, but because Surkhet's true tail
(ξ = 0.398, sample skewness 2.95) is considerably heavier than the log-normal example Day 1 used.
Same structural mistake (thin-tailed model, heavy-tailed reality), a more extreme instance of it.
</details>

**I5.** Your colleague says: *"We should just always use L-moments instead of MLE for GEV — it's
strictly better."* Using both C5 and the Set D3/G5 findings, write a two-sentence pushback.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Solution</b></summary>

A model answer — yours should differ in wording, not in substance:

> L-moments' real advantage is narrower bootstrap-resampling variance, not universal robustness —
> Pokhara's MLE and L-moments fits disagree in *sign* despite both having small |ξ|, and the

> deletion. The correct comparison is bootstrap CI width under each estimator, not a blanket
> preference — which is exactly why that comparison, not yet run for L-moments, is the open thread.
</details>
