2Independence is a special case, not the default

The multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A) is always true — it is the definition of a conditional probability, rearranged. The independence rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) is true only when P(B | A) happens to equal P(B). One of these is a law; the other is an assumption you are making about the world.

And Bayes is not a new idea. The same joint probability factors two ways — rain first, or saturation first:

P(R) · P(S | R)  =   =  P(R ∩ S)  =   =  P(S) · P(R | S)
Set the two outer expressions equal, divide, and you have P(R | S) = P(S | R) · P(R) / P(S). That is Bayes' theorem — an algebraic rearrangement, not a separate law about belief.

3The probability integral transform

Now the continuous case, and the one trick everything downstream rests on. Take any continuous variable X and push it through its own cdf: U = F(X). The result is Uniform(0,1) — always, whatever shape went in. Pick a margin and watch.

Why this is the load-bearing step: the three margins have nothing in common — different families, different units, different skew, one of them bounded on both sides. After each passes through its own cdf they are indistinguishable. That is what "map marginal CDFs to copula input structure" means, and it is the reason a copula can put rainfall in millimetres, discharge in m³/s and soil saturation as a bare fraction into the same model without any of them needing the same shape. 4Sklar: the margins and the dependence come apart

Sklar's theorem says any joint distribution splits into margins and a copula, and that the two can be chosen independently: F(q,v) = C(FQ(q), FV(v)). The claim is easier to believe when you can change one and watch the other refuse to move. Change either control below.

Watch the two controls do different things. Switch the margins and the left panel distorts beyond recognition while the right panel does not move a single point — the ranks are untouched, so the copula cannot tell. Switch the copula and both panels change, because the dependence structure is genuinely different data. That asymmetry is Sklar's theorem, and it is why this project can argue about GEV-versus-LP3 and about Gumbel-versus-Gaussian as two separate arguments instead of one tangled one.

This is P(B | A) from Panel 1, evaluated in the tail. Nothing new has been introduced — the same conditional probability, the same definition, asked at the edge of the distribution instead of the middle. λU is its limit as u → 1. And note Clayton: it has strong tail dependence, worth 0.707, but in the lower corner, where two variables being simultaneously small is what binds. For a drought study that is the right family. For a flood cascade it is as wrong as the Gaussian, and wrong in a way that a τ of 0.5 conceals completely.

6What it costs — AND, OR, and the chain

The payoff. Once there are two variables, "the 100-year event" stops being a single number, because there are two different failures you might mean. And once there are three links, the choice of dependence model is worth three orders of magnitude.

AND and OR are not two estimates of one quantity. They are answers to two different engineering questions. A culvert that fails when the peak overtops it, regardless of volume, is an OR problem. A reservoir that fails only when a high peak arrives and the volume is large enough to keep it high is an AND problem. Quoting one when the mechanism is the other is a category error, not a conservatism.

And now the cascade. Chain three links, each at u = 0.99. Multiply the probabilities as though the links were independent and you get one in a million years. Chain them through λU = 0.586 instead and you get one in 291. The independence assumption is not a little conservative and not a little wrong — it is out by a factor of 3,431, and it is wrong in the direction that makes a structure look safe. Every link you add multiplies the error again, which is precisely why a cascading hazard is where this matters and a single-hazard analysis can often get away with it.
