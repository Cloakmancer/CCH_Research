5

Anscombe's four twins

Same statistics, four different stories

Four datasets, constructed in 1973 by the statistician Francis Anscombe. Switch between them — mean of x, mean of y, r, the regression line and R² barely move. What moves is everything a summary statistic can't see.

nmean xmean y rslopeinterceptR²
The lesson isn't "regression is broken." It's that r, the slope and R² are each a single number chosen to summarise an entire cloud of points, and any single number can be hit by more than one cloud. The fix isn't a better statistic — it's plot the data, every time, before you trust the summary.

Six things worth keeping

  • i Correlation and simple regression are the same relationship in two units — r is the slope once both variables are standardised.
  • ii A single point far from the average on x can swing the whole line. That power is called leverage, and it doesn't care whether the point is trustworthy.
  • iii Eyeballing a correlation is a skill with a learning curve, not an innate sense — most people's first guesses are overconfident about weak clouds.
  • iv Least-squares regression is a specific optimisation, not a law of nature: the line that minimises total squared vertical distance to the points.
  • v Identical r, slope and R² can hide a straight line, a curve, an outlier, or a single lever. Always plot before you trust the summary.
  • vi A strong r never tells you which way the arrow points, or whether there's an arrow at all — a third variable can drive two others without either touching the other.
